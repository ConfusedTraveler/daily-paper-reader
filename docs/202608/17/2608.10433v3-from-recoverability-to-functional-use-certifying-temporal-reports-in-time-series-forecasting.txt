Abstract 

Models increasingly accompany time-series forecasts with temporal reports—delays, leading indicators, or selected history—yet a correct report need not describe the computation that produced the forecast. We formalize this as a three-stage certification problem: recoverability of the target from the realized trajectory, correctness of the model’s report, and functional use of the reported history. For Gaussian point delays, we derive an exact finite-sample recovery–substitutability identity: the same realized shift geometry yields structural evidence at scale nη n but normalized proxy-prediction cost at scale ηn. Thus a delay can be decisively identifiable while a correlated alternative remains near-oracle. We then audit intentionally unconstrained TCN and N-HiTS forecasters only on trajectories that are recoverable, correctly reported, and near-oracle. Even there, Jacobian and in-distribution conditional-replacement response peaks lie 9.3–13 .5 time steps from the reported delay. Finally, a report-conditioned no-bypass factorization provides a sufficient access certificate; architecture-matched post-hoc routing and gate-destruction controls show that the alignment change is attributable to report-coordinate access. The framework distinguishes evidence that a temporal statement is identifiable and correct from evidence that the forecast computation actually depends on it. 

# 1 Introduction 

Temporal structure is increasingly exposed alongside forecasts: a system may name a delay, leading indicator, selected history window, or distributed temporal pattern in addition to producing a numerical prediction. Such reports carry two different kinds of information. They may identify a property that is supported by the observed trajectory, and they may describe the computation that actually produced the forecast. Treating these claims as interchangeable turns a readable structural output into a stronger computational certificate than the evidence warrants. This paper develops the statistical and functional conditions needed to separate them. Throughout, the neural systems are forecasting backbones equipped with an explicit temporal-structure report ; the report is an evaluated model output rather than an assumed property of a vanilla TCN or N-HiTS. We organize temporal-report evidence into a three-stage certificate hierarchy. Recoverability asks whether the realized trajectory contains enough information to distinguish the target structure from alternatives. Report correctness asks whether the fitted system names that structure correctly. Functional use asks whether the numerical prediction depends on the history named by the report. The stages are evaluated in the order recoverability −→ report correctness −→ functional use ,

with each stage supplying evidence of a different kind. Autocorrelation makes the hierarchy consequential: nearby lags can be predictive substitutes, output memory can compensate for an incorrect input delay, and accurate forecasts can be supported by history different from the generating mechanism (Bjorklund and Ljung, 2003; Sadler and Kozick, 2006; Ljung, 1999; Seborg et al., 2016; Kay, 1993). A temporal 1arXiv:2608.10433v3 [cs.LG] 14 Aug 2026 report is therefore computationally informative only when structural evidence is connected to the forecast map itself. The theoretical core identifies the statistical reason that this connection cannot be inferred from structural correctness alone. For point delays, structural discrimination and predictive substitution are governed by the same realized shift geometry , but that geometry enters the two problems at different scales. If ηn denotes the normalized prediction penalty of a wrong-lag proxy, trajectory-level structural evidence grows with nη n while proxy prediction cost remains at scale ηn. This yields an exact finite-sample certificate gap: the true delay can be statistically decisive while a correlated alternative remains within a fixed near-oracle tolerance. In the local-substitutability regime, structural error can vanish even as the proxy excess risk itself tends to zero. The fixed-process and local asymptotic consequences make this separation a property of the statistical problem, not a peculiarity of a particular neural architecture. The empirical study instantiates the same hierarchy in learned forecasters. The primary Link-II evaluation fixes the first two stages and forecast quality simultaneously by restricting to P1 trajectories with Bayes risk at most .05 , a correct explicit report, and one-step normalized error NER ≤ .10 . On these rows, TCN- and N-HiTS-based systems still place their dominant numerical dependence far from the reported delay under local Jacobians and conditional replacement from the exact white/AR(1) Gaussian input law. Crucially, the separate-head contract is not proposed as a faithful architecture: the realized report is deliberately not fed into the forecast. Its role is to test whether recoverability, report correctness, and forecast accuracy alone constitute evidence of functional use. Feeding the report into the predictor would instead assume the final link that the audit is designed to evaluate. 

Contributions. Our contributions are threefold. 1. A certificate hierarchy for temporal reports. We formalize recoverability, report correctness, and functional use as distinct evidential objects, giving temporal explanation a statistical-to-computational evaluation order rather than a single accuracy criterion. 2. An exact recovery–substitutability law. For point delays we derive a finite-sample identity, together with fixed-process and local-asymptotic consequences, showing that the same realized temporal geometry controls structural identification at scale nη n and predictive substitutability at scale ηn.3. An empirical witness and a sufficient access certificate. Separate-head forecasters show that recoverability, a correct report, and near-oracle prediction do not by themselves certify func-tional use. A no-bypass factorization then makes report-conditioned access a verifiable property; architecture-matched multi-seed, post-hoc, and gate-destruction controls isolate coordinate access as the mechanism driving alignment. 

Central principle. The resulting principle is that statistical evidence and computational evidence answer different questions. Recoverability certifies that a structural question is answerable from the observed trajectory; report correctness certifies that the fitted system answers that question correctly; functional-use analysis certifies how that answer is connected to the numerical forecast. The recovery– substitutability identity links the first and third stages through a common geometry while proving that their statistical scales differ. The no-bypass construction supplies the complementary computational certificate by making access through the reported structure explicit. Together, these pieces turn temporal reporting from a single-output interpretation problem into a staged theory of what can be inferred about a forecaster.

# 2 Related Work 

Recoverability and temporal structure. Delay and lead–lag estimation are classical in system identifi-cation and remain central in modern time-series analysis (Bjorklund and Ljung, 2003; Sadler and Kozick, 2006; Ljung, 1999; Ma and Huang, 2023; Cattaldo et al., 2024; Heyse et al., 2021; Zhao and Shen, 2024; Runge et al., 2019a,b; Runge, 2020). Recent work studies when temporal structure is identifiable or recoverable from the observed process (Kuskova et al., 2026b; Tan et al., 2025). We use recoverability as a trajectory-level certificate: complete candidate likelihoods give input-conditioned Bayes limits for P1/P3, while nuisance-profile separation handles P2 settings in which an incorrect lag can refit autoregressive dynamics. This places structural reporting on top of an explicit statistical information limit. 

Faithfulness and temporal explanations. A readable model output and the computation producing a prediction are distinct objects in the broader faithfulness literature (Jain and Wallace, 2019; Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020; Ross et al., 2017). Time-series explanation methods use perturbations, context windows, predictive masks, information bottlenecks, and forecast-necessity criteria to characterize temporal dependence (Ozyegen et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024b,a; Kim et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Kuskova et al., 2026a; Zheng et al., 2026); autocorrelation can distort marginal temporal attribution (Tunyi, 2026). Our setting links this functional question to a separately evaluated structural claim. Conditional replacement under the known Gaussian input law complements masking, finite perturbations, and Jacobians by measuring the same report–forecast relation under an in-distribution intervention. 

Predictive multiplicity and alternative computations. Predictive performance need not determine a unique rule: underspecified pipelines can return validation-equivalent predictors with different behavior (D’Amour et al., 2022), predictive multiplicity studies near-equivalent models that disagree on predictions (Marx et al., 2020), and shortcut learning shows that benchmark success can rely on unintended decision rules (Geirhos et al., 2020). We study a temporal, directly auditable instance in which the structural claim is explicit and recoverability is computed from the realized trajectory. 

Bottlenecks and controlled benchmarks. Concept bottlenecks and related inherently faithful archi-tectures place an interpretable variable on the prediction path (Koh et al., 2020). We use the same architectural idea as a computational certificate : the no-bypass factorization bounds off-report access, and matched post-hoc and gate-destruction controls identify coordinate access as the operative mechanism. Controlled synthetic and causal benchmarks provide the known structural targets required to score both 3statistical recovery and model response (Cheng et al., 2024; Mogensen et al., 2024; Stein et al., 2025; Herdeanu et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). 

Closest boundaries and our scope. Closest to Stage I, recoverability work asks whether neural temporal interactions are identifiable from observed support and develops pre-fit diagnostics (Kuskova et al., 2026b); it does not evaluate correctness or use of a separate explicit report. Closest to the final stage, forecast-necessity testing asks whether removing a candidate interaction degrades a nonlinear forecaster (Kuskova et al., 2026a); it does not condition necessity on an input-conditioned recovery limit and a correct report. Existing time-series explainers likewise characterize a fitted predictor without jointly separating these three questions. We connect them through the point-delay scale law, a conditioned learned-model witness, and a no-bypass condition for verifiable report-coordinate access.

# 4 Theory 

The theory develops the two certificates needed by the hierarchy. The statistical certificate is determined by how strongly the realized trajectory separates structural candidates; predictive substitutability is determined by the cost of replacing the target history with an alternative. The central identity shows that these quantities share one geometry but use different normalizations, creating a regime in which structural identification is decisive while proxy prediction remains near-oracle. The second part formalizes a computational access certificate: a report-conditioned no-bypass factorization converts the final report– use relation into a property of the prediction map. Full statements and proofs are collected in Sections B and C.

# 5 Experiments 

The experiments evaluate the certificate hierarchy in the same order as the theory. TCN (Bai et al., 2018) and N-HiTS (Challu et al., 2023) are the primary backbones, each equipped with the explicit structural report defined in Section 3; matched statistical references quantify recoverability for each mechanism. The unified cohort contains 400 trajectories. For P1 the declared candidate set is D = {2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 },and every report–use “gap” is measured in raw historical time-step lag units, not in candidate-index distance. The architecture-matched access study uses an independent 1,200-trajectory P1 cohort and ten fixed training seeds, and the P2 mechanism replication uses a second independent 1,200-trajectory cohort. All paired uncertainty preserves trajectory identity, and crossed seed–trajectory rows are treated as repeated evaluations rather than independent samples.

# 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

Temporal reporting admits a natural certificate hierarchy: the data must support a structural distinction, the fitted system must report that structure correctly, and the numerical forecast must be connected to the reported history. The recovery–substitutability identity supplies the statistical law linking these stages. Because the same realized temporal geometry appears as nη n in structural evidence and ηn

in normalized proxy cost, statistical certainty about a delay can coexist with inexpensive predictive substitution. Structural correctness and computational necessity are therefore different properties even when they arise from the same observed trajectory. The learned-model results provide a witness to this certificate gap rather than a prevalence claim about all reporting architectures. On trajectories that are recoverable, correctly reported, and near-oracle in one-step prediction, separate-head TCN- and N-HiTS-based systems retain large report–use gaps under Jacobian and in-distribution conditional-replacement audits. The no-bypass factorization supplies the complementary computational side of the argument: report-conditioned access makes off-report sensitivity explicitly controllable within the declared historical block. Architecture-matched multi-seed comparisons, within-checkpoint gating, gate destruction, and an autoregressive-delay replication localize the observed alignment change to that access mechanism. Candidate, input, and prior contract. The candidate index H is independent of the input mechanism in the core synthetic tier: U ⊥ H. Exact Bayes statements condition on every boundary variable used by the candidate likelihood. If an input policy or initial-history law depends on the candidate, the structural prior is conditioned on the observed variables and the corresponding likelihood contribution is included. P2 profile calibration uses the common-information window in Equation (1), which excludes the transition whose burn-in boundary value is not part of the observed history. 

Innovation contract. P1 and P3 assume independent Gaussian innovations. P2 assumes ϵt is indepen-dent of the filtration generated by past output and the observed exogenous input. Closed-loop inputs are excluded from the exact P2 theorem unless the controller and likelihood are explicitly modeled. 

Boundary history and the P2 common-information window. The general P2/P3 conditional formulas require the boundary output history used by the recursion to be observed and conditioned upon consistently. For P2, the first scored transition depends on a burn-in boundary value that is not part of the serialized observed history. The common-information analysis therefore starts at L+2 , where YL+1 is observed, and uses nP2 = T − L − 1. This is the declared evaluation window for all reported P2 profile calibration. The implemented exact P3 cohort has a = 0 , so no output-boundary history enters its KL/Bayes computation. For a future nonzero-a exact P3 cohort, the common observed boundary history would have to be conditioned upon explicitly. 

Known versus unknown nuisance parameters. A known-parameter Bayes oracle defines a strict same-information limit only when those parameters are fixed and included in the observed information set for every evaluated system. If the parameters are hidden or vary within the scenario, the known-parameter result is a labeled lower reference. Profile likelihood is a feasible reference when gain, previous-output carry-over, or variance are unknown. Profile-KL is a population separation diagnostic for a composite incorrect candidate; it is not, by itself, an exact composite-hypothesis Bayes risk. 

Model/report computational contract. For the separate-head neural evaluation, the scientific contract is functional rather than architecture-name specific: the numerical forecast is computed without taking the realized report r or a report-induced historical gate as an input. The report is a declarative estimate of temporal structure, not an instruction that must be fed back into the predictor. A report head may share upstream features or parameters with the forecaster, but changing the report value while holding the forecast-path state fixed does not alter the forecast forward pass. In routed regimes, the report induces the gate controlling access within the audited historical block as in Equation (9). These two contracts instantiate, respectively, the unconstrained report–use test and a block-conditional no-bypass certificate. For every separate-head P1–P3 run reported from the unified release, TCN and N-HiTS use Lsep =0.1 MSE( by, y ) + CE( z, c ): one-step forecast MSE has weight 0.1 and report cross-entropy has weight 

1.0. AdamW uses learning rate 10 −3, batch size 128, 12 epochs, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Both losses update the shared backbone; only MSE updates the scalar forecast head, and only cross-entropy updates the report head. Thus the report objective can improve structural reporting without placing the realized report upstream of the forecast. This lack of a graph-level implication is the intended null contract for the certification test, not a claim that the architecture is inherently faithful.

# F Detailed Experimental Sampling Protocol 

F.1 Difficulty-targeted sampling 

For exact-tier instances, a proposal is repeatedly generated until its oracle quantity falls in the target bin. The proposal distribution and maximum rejection count are fixed before benchmark execution. If a bin cannot be populated without extreme parameters, the bin is marked unsupported rather than filled by changing its definition. 

F.2 Minimum reporting units 

The statistical unit is a complete trajectory. Training seeds are nested within model configurations and trajectories are nested within scenarios. Scenario-level summaries are reported alongside pooled trajectory metrics to prevent large easy scenario families from dominating the leaderboard. 

Crossed seed–trajectory comparisons. G.2 Functional-use diagnostics and the strict Link-II audit 

Operational meaning of functional use. Throughout the paper, “use” denotes controlled sensitivity of the fitted numerical predictor to historical input locations under a declared intervention. It is not a claim of real-world causality or complete information provenance. The primary point-delay summaries are the distance from the reported lag to the response peak and the fraction of peaks equal to the report; 25 the peak is taken over the coordinates audited in the corresponding model, which for Fresh-B are the five candidate-supported lags. Local input Jacobians provide a differential audit, while conditional replacement draws from the exact stationary white/AR(1) Gaussian conditional and therefore keeps the replacement intervention in distribution. Zero masking and finite additive perturbations are secondary checks rather than alternative definitions of ground truth. Before interpreting learned-model scores, we test the diagnostics on predictors with known functional structure, including true-delay, deliberately shifted, two-path, output-only, uniform-history, and hard one-hot rules. Additive sensitivity and Jacobians recover the nonuniform known profiles with zero peak error, and all diagnostics reject input use for the output-only rule. Conditional donor replacement localizes single-path rules to within roughly one lag on average but is less reliable for weak secondary paths, which is why the main full-profile interpretation relies on Jacobian/additive audits and uses conditional replacement as an in-distribution point-delay check.
