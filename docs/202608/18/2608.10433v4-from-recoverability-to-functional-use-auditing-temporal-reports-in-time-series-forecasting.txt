Abstract 

Time-series forecasters increasingly accompany numerical predictions with explicit temporal reports, such as delays or selected history, but a correct report need not describe the information actually used by the forecast. We separate this problem into three questions: whether the target temporal structure is recoverable from the observed trajectory, whether the model reports it correctly, and whether the forecast functionally depends on the reported history. For point delays, we show that accurate prediction does not imply correct temporal use: two candidate delays can become statistically distinguishable as the trajectory grows even when substituting one for the other incurs little per-step prediction error. On controlled time-series tasks, learned forecasters can remain primarily dependent on history outside the reported structure even when the target is recoverable, the report is correct, and the forecast is accurate. Finally, report-controlled routing substantially improves report–forecast alignment without an observed loss in predictive accuracy. These results motivate separate statistical and functional validation of temporal reports. 

# 1 Introduction 

Temporal structure is increasingly exposed alongside forecasts: a system may name a delay, selected history window, or distributed temporal pattern in addition to producing a numerical prediction. Such reports carry two different kinds of information. They may identify a property that is supported by the observed trajectory, and they may describe the computation that actually produced the forecast. Treating these claims as interchangeable gives a structural report a stronger interpretation than the evidence supports. This paper develops the statistical and functional conditions needed to separate them. We organize temporal-report evaluation into three stages. Recoverability asks whether the realized tra-jectory contains enough information to distinguish the target structure from alternatives. Report correctness 

asks whether the fitted system names that structure correctly. Functional use asks whether the numerical prediction depends on the history named by the report. The stages are evaluated in the order recoverability −→ report correctness −→ functional use ,

with each stage supplying evidence of a different kind. Autocorrelation makes the hierarchy consequential: nearby lags can be predictive substitutes, output memory can compensate for an incorrect input delay, and accurate forecasts can be supported by history different from the generating mechanism (Bjorklund and Ljung, 2003; Sadler and Kozick, 2006; Ljung, 1999; Seborg et al., 2016; Kay, 1993). A temporal report is therefore computationally informative only when structural evidence is connected to the forecast map itself. The theoretical core clarifies why structural recoverability alone cannot establish functional use. In the point-delay setting, the evidence for distinguishing two candidate delays accumulates over the observed 1arXiv:2608.10433v4 [cs.LG] 17 Aug 2026 trajectory, whereas the prediction penalty associated with substituting one lag for another is measured per observation. If ηn denotes the normalized excess prediction risk of such a substitution, then the trajectory-level evidence for distinguishing the two delays scales as nη n, while the per-observation excess prediction risk scales as ηn. Structural evidence and predictive consequences therefore need not strengthen at the same rate with sample size. Recovering the true delay from a trajectory is therefore not sufficient to determine whether, or how, a forecaster’s numerical prediction depends on that delay. To evaluate this hierarchy empirically, we construct controlled time-series tasks in which the ground-truth temporal structure is known and its recoverability can be quantified for each realized trajectory. We equip forecasting models with an explicit temporal report and evaluate the three stages separately. We first identify trajectories for which the target structure is statistically recoverable, and then ask whether the model reports that structure correctly while maintaining accurate forecasts. Only after these conditions are satisfied do we examine functional use, by measuring how the numerical prediction responds when different parts of the input history are locally perturbed or replaced by values drawn consistently with the input process. We find that a model can recover and correctly report the target structure, and predict accurately, while its forecast remains primarily dependent on history other than that named by the report. We further compare this unconstrained setting with a matched predictor whose access to candidate history is explicitly routed through the reported structure. This constraint substantially improves the alignment between the report and the forecast computation without sacrificing predictive accuracy; in some model–task settings, forecast error even decreases.

# 2 Related Work 

Our work connects three areas: recovering temporal structure from observed time series, explaining the tem-poral dependence of learned forecasters, and constraining predictive models to use interpretable intermediate structure. We discuss each area below and clarify how our setting differs from existing work. 

Recoverability and temporal structure. Delay and lead–lag estimation are classical problems in system identification and remain important in modern time-series analysis (Bjorklund and Ljung, 2003; Sadler and Kozick, 2006; Ljung, 1999; Ma and Huang, 2023; Cattaldo et al., 2024; Heyse et al., 2021; Zhao and Shen, 2024; Runge et al., 2019a,b; Runge, 2020). More recent work asks when a temporal relation reported by a flexible neural model is actually identifiable from the observed input support. In particular, Kuskova et al. (2026b) study interaction recoverability under dependent lagged inputs and develop a pre-fit diagnostic for when the data support a reported interaction. This question is closely related to our first stage, but our structural target is specified independently of the learned forecaster as a finite delay or temporal kernel. We use recoverability only as the entrance condition for the subsequent evaluation: after determining whether the realized trajectory distinguishes the target from alternatives, we separately test whether a model reports that target correctly and whether its numerical forecast depends on the corresponding history. 

Faithfulness and temporal dependence in forecasting. A model’s interpretable output and the computation that produces its prediction need not coincide, a distinction studied broadly in the literature on explanation faithfulness (Jain and Wallace, 2019; Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020; Ross et al., 2017). Time-series methods use perturbations, sparse temporal masks, selected context, and information bottlenecks to identify influential history (Ozyegen et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024c,b; Zhang et al., 2025). Recent work makes the connection to prediction more explicit in several ways. Delta-XAI evaluates the faithfulness and sufficiency of explanations for changing time-series predictions (Kim et al., 2026); IB-Forecast learns explicit input masks intended to provide faithful forecasting explanations (Zheng et al., 2026); and forecast-necessity testing removes candidate temporal relations and asks whether they are required to maintain forecast accuracy (Kuskova et al., 2026a). These questions are closely related to our functional analysis, but our audit first fixes a recoverable and correctly reported temporal target before testing whether the forecast depends on that history. Forecast-necessity methods ask whether removing a relation degrades prediction, whereas we measure where the fitted forecast is functionally sensitive. Temporal dependence further complicates both types of analysis 3because autocorrelation can make marginal attribution unreliable (Tunyi, 2026). Related observations arise from underspecification, predictive multiplicity, and shortcut learning, where models with similar predictive performance can rely on different decision rules (D’Amour et al., 2022; Marx et al., 2020; Geirhos et al., 2020). 

Constraining models to use interpretable structure. Concept bottleneck models and related architectures place interpretable variables directly on the prediction path, thereby making the relationship between an intermediate representation and the final prediction explicit (Koh et al., 2020). We use the same general idea only as a controlled comparison rather than proposing a new bottleneck architecture: an unconstrained predictor is matched against a predictor whose access to candidate history is controlled by its temporal report, allowing us to isolate how historical access changes report–forecast agreement and forecasting accuracy. Controlled synthetic and causal time-series benchmarks provide complementary settings in which the underlying temporal or causal structure is known (Cheng et al., 2024; Mogensen et al., 2024; Stein et al., 2025; Herdeanu et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). SynTSBench similarly uses programmable temporal patterns to evaluate what forecasting models learn from controlled data (Tan et al., 2026). Our experiments use controlled processes for a narrower purpose: to keep the target structure, its recoverability, the model report, and the history used by the numerical forecast separately measurable.

4.2 Structural evidence and prediction error scale differently 

The previous subsection asks whether the observed trajectory contains enough information to distinguish the true delay from alternative candidates. We now ask a related but different question: if a predictor uses an alternative delay, how much prediction accuracy is actually lost? These two questions need not become easier or harder at the same rate as the trajectory length increases. 7The proposition shows that these two quantities can behave very differently as the trajectory becomes longer. The statistical evidence for distinguishing the true delay from an alternative accumulates across observations and therefore depends on nη n, whereas the excess prediction error incurred by using the alternative lag is measured per observation and depends only on ηn. Consequently, two delays can become increasingly easy to distinguish statistically while the prediction loss associated with using the wrong lag remains small. Prediction accuracy alone therefore cannot determine whether a predictor relies on the correct delay. In particular, when ηn → 0 but nη n → ∞ , the structural identification error tends to zero even though the excess prediction error of the alternative lag also tends to zero. 

# 5 Report-Controlled Historical Access 

The preceding analysis shows that recoverability, report correctness, and prediction accuracy do not determine which historical inputs a forecaster actually uses. We therefore study a simple architectural intervention in which the temporal report controls direct access to the candidate historical inputs. Let r denote the temporal report and let g(r) be the corresponding gate over the candidate history u. We consider predictors of the form 

F (u, v ; r) = G v, r, g(r) ⊙ u, (9) where v contains additional context available to the predictor and G receives no other copy of u. By construction, a coordinate with gk(r) = 0 cannot directly affect the forecast through this historical block; for a one-hot point-delay report, only the reported lag is directly available to the predictor. This construction is not claimed to be the unique or optimal way to obtain report–forecast agreement. Rather, it provides a controlled intervention for testing whether explicitly connecting the reported temporal structure to the prediction path changes which historical inputs the forecast depends on. The experiments below compare this routed predictor with an otherwise matched unconstrained predictor and use post-hoc routing and report–coordinate destruction controls to isolate the effect of this change in historical access. 

# 6 Experiments 

The experiments address three questions in sequence. First, does forecast accuracy provide the same information as recovery of the underlying temporal structure? Second, when the structure is recoverable, the model reports it correctly, and the numerical forecast is accurate, does the forecast actually depend on the reported history? Third, if access to candidate history is explicitly controlled by the report, does report–forecast alignment improve without sacrificing predictive accuracy? TCN (Bai et al., 2018) and N-HiTS (Challu et al., 2023) are used as the primary forecasting backbones. The common-cohort comparison also includes a lightweight MLP-NARX forecaster with a frozen structural probe, and an independently trained iTransformer (Liu et al., 2024a) replication is also reported to test whether the same qualitative separation appears with a Transformer backbone. The first two primary questions are evaluated on a common 400-trajectory cohort; the iTransformer replication uses an independent 1,200-trajectory cohort per task with three training seeds; and the routing study uses independent 1,200-trajectory P1 and P2 cohorts with ten fixed training seeds. Independent cohorts are not pooled for model ranking. All paired uncertainty preserves trajectory identity, and crossed seed–trajectory rows are treated as repeated evaluations rather than independent samples.

# 7 Discussion and Conclusion 

Our results separate three questions that are often conflated in temporal reporting: whether a structure is recoverable from the observed trajectory, whether the model reports it correctly, and whether the numerical forecast actually depends on the reported history. The point-delay analysis shows why prediction accuracy alone cannot resolve the last question: a model may incur little predictive penalty from using an alternative lag even when the true delay is statistically well identified. The experiments demonstrate the same separation in learned forecasters, where recoverable and correctly reported delays can coexist with accurate predictions whose dominant dependence lies elsewhere in the history. This distinction has a direct implication for how temporal models should be analyzed. A temporal report should not be evaluated only by whether it matches a known structure, nor should forecast accuracy be taken as evidence that the reported structure is functionally used. Model evaluation should instead separate what the data support, what the model reports, and what the prediction function actually uses. Report-controlled routing further shows that these properties need not remain disconnected: explicitly linking historical access to the reported structure can substantially improve report–forecast alignment without an observed loss in predictive accuracy. More broadly, these results suggest a design principle for future time-series models with interpretable temporal outputs. Temporal explanations can be treated not only as auxiliary predictions to be evaluated after forecasting, but also as structural variables that organize how the predictor accesses history. This creates a path toward models whose temporal reports are both statistically supported and computationally meaningful, and toward benchmarks that evaluate forecasting accuracy together with the reliability of the temporal structure exposed by the model. 11 AI use statement

Candidate, input, and prior contract. The candidate index H is independent of the input mechanism in the core synthetic tier: U ⊥ H. If an input policy or initial-history law depends on the candidate, the structural prior is conditioned on the observed variables and the corresponding likelihood contribution is included. P2 profile calibration uses the common-information window in Equation (2), which excludes the transition whose burn-in boundary value is not part of the observed history. 

Innovation contract. P1 and P3 assume independent Gaussian innovations. P2 assumes ϵt is independent of the filtration generated by past output and the observed exogenous input. Closed-loop inputs are excluded from the exact P2 theorem unless the controller and likelihood are explicitly modeled. 

Boundary history and the P2 common-information window. The general P2/P3 conditional formulas require the boundary output history used by the recursion to be observed and conditioned upon consistently. For P2, the first scored transition depends on a burn-in boundary value that is not part of the serialized observed history. The common-information analysis therefore starts at L + 2 , where YL+1 is observed, and uses nP2 = T − L − 1. This is the declared evaluation window for all reported P2 profile calibration. The implemented exact P3 cohort has a = 0 , so no output-boundary history enters its KL/Bayes computation. For a future nonzero-a exact P3 cohort, the common observed boundary history would have to be conditioned upon explicitly. 

Known versus unknown nuisance parameters. A known-parameter Bayes oracle defines a strict same-information limit only when those parameters are fixed and included in the observed information set for every evaluated system. If the parameters are hidden or vary within the scenario, the known-parameter result is a labeled lower reference. Profile likelihood is a feasible reference when gain, previous-output carry-over, or variance are unknown. Profile-KL is a population separation diagnostic for a composite incorrect candidate; it is not, by itself, an exact composite-hypothesis Bayes risk. 

Model/report computational contract. For the separate-head neural evaluation, the scientific contract is functional rather than architecture-name specific: the numerical forecast is computed without taking the realized report r or a report-induced historical gate as an input. The report is a declarative estimate of temporal structure, not an instruction that must be fed back into the predictor. A report head may share upstream features or parameters with the forecaster, but changing the report value while holding the forecast-path state fixed does not alter the forecast forward pass. In routed regimes, the report induces the gate controlling access within the audited historical block as in Equation (9). These two contracts instantiate, respectively, the unconstrained report–use test and the report-controlled historical-access condition. For every separate-head P1–P3 run reported from the unified release, TCN and N-HiTS use Lsep =0.1 MSE( by, y ) + CE( z, c ): one-step forecast MSE has weight 0.1 and report cross-entropy has weight 1.0.AdamW uses learning rate 10 −3, batch size 128, 12 epochs, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Both losses update the shared backbone; only MSE updates the scalar forecast head, and only cross-entropy updates the report head. Thus the report objective can improve structural reporting without placing the realized report upstream of the forecast. B.3 Direct consequences of report-controlled historical access 

The routing construction in Equation (9) has a few direct consequences that are useful for interpreting the intervention experiments. Fix the report r and the additional context v. For any perturbation ∆u to the candidate history, 

F (u + ∆ u, v ; r) = G(v, r, g(r) ⊙ (u + ∆ u)) .

If ∆u is supported only on coordinates for which gk(r) = 0 , then 

g(r) ⊙ (u + ∆ u) = g(r) ⊙ u, 

and the forecast is unchanged. Thus the routing construction removes direct sensitivity to historical coordi-nates that receive zero gate weight. The same calculation gives a quantitative statement for soft gates. If G is LG-Lipschitz with respect to its gated-history argument and |gk(r)| ≤ τ on every coordinate in the support of ∆u, then 

∥F (u + ∆ u, v ; r) − F (u, v ; r)∥ ≤ LG∥g(r) ⊙ ∆u∥2

≤ LGτ ∥∆u∥2.

For a one-hot point-delay gate g(r) = er, every perturbation away from the reported coordinate therefore has zero direct effect through the routed history block. If the reported coordinate itself has nonzero intervention response, it is the unique response peak within that block. These statements concern direct access through the candidate history u while r and v are held fixed. They do not claim that all information about past inputs has been removed from other observed context. In P2, for example, v includes the observed autoregressive context Yt−1, which may itself carry information about earlier inputs. If an intervention also changes the report or the additional context, the resulting response includes those paths as well.

E.1 Unified generation order 

For each trajectory, the implementation follows the fixed order: 1. sample the input family and generate U1: Traw ;2. sample the structural candidate and dynamic nuisance parameters; 3. generate the clean latent process and apply burn-in removal; 4. generate innovations using an independent random stream; 5. crop every candidate to the common task window; for P2 profile calibration, additionally drop the first post-boundary transition and use IP2

E.4 Generator validation 

Each configuration must pass: • stability and finite-value checks; • target versus empirical SNR checks; • candidate-label independence from input metadata and array shape; • empirical autocorrelation and spectrum sanity checks; • impulse-response verification for every mechanism family; • paired-noise reproducibility and random-seed replay; • shortcut tests using input-only and metadata-only classifiers. F.1 Difficulty-targeted sampling 

For exact-tier instances, a proposal is repeatedly generated until its oracle quantity falls in the target bin. The proposal distribution and maximum rejection count are fixed before benchmark execution. If a bin cannot be populated without extreme parameters, the bin is marked unsupported rather than filled by changing its definition. 

F.2 Minimum reporting units 

The statistical unit is a complete trajectory. Training seeds are nested within model configurations and trajectories are nested within scenarios. Scenario-level summaries are reported alongside pooled trajectory metrics to prevent large easy scenario families from dominating the leaderboard. 

Crossed seed–trajectory comparisons. Fresh-B evaluates the same frozen test trajectories under multiple independently trained seeds, so the resulting seed ×trajectory table is a crossed repeated-evaluation design rather than a collection of independent rows. Scientific conclusions therefore do not use the raw number of seed–trajectory rows as an independent sample size. We report seed-level effects and direction counts alongside paired interval estimates, and all model contrasts keep trajectory identity paired. The exact frozen resampling implementation and bootstrap seeds are part of the released analysis artifacts.

Operational meaning of functional use. Throughout the paper, “use” denotes controlled sensitivity of the fitted numerical predictor to historical input locations under a declared intervention. It is not a claim of real-world causality or complete information provenance. The primary point-delay summaries are the distance from the reported lag to the response peak and the fraction of peaks equal to the report. Local input Jacobians provide a differential audit, while the second primary intervention replaces a candidate history value by a draw from its exact stationary white/AR(1) Gaussian conditional distribution, keeping the replacement in distribution. Zero masking and finite additive perturbations are secondary checks rather than alternative definitions of ground truth. Before interpreting learned-model scores, we test the diagnostics on predictors with known functional structure, including true-delay, deliberately shifted, two-path, output-only, uniform-history, and hard one-hot rules. Additive sensitivity and Jacobians recover the nonuniform known profiles with zero peak error, and all diagnostics reject input use for the output-only rule. Replacing history values by draws from the Gaussian conditional distribution localizes single-path rules to within roughly one lag on average but is less reliable for weak secondary paths, which is why the main full-profile interpretation relies on Jacobian/additive audits and uses this intervention only as an in-distribution point-delay check.
