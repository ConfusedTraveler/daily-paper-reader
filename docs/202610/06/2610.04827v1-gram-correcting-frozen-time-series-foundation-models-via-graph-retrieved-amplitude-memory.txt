ABSTRACT 

Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) enable zero-shot forecasting through large-scale cross-domain pretraining, while retrieval augmentation further im-proves their performance by leveraging historical information. However, exist-ing methods typically correct TSFM forecasts using the ground-truth futures of similar historical windows, which contain both predictive components already captured by the foundation model and sample-specific random fluctuation that is difficult to transfer. In contrast, recurring systematic model bias within pre-diction errors more directly characterizes the failure modes of a frozen TSFM and therefore provides more valuable correction signals. Effectively exploiting such model bias, however, poses two challenges: prediction errors at different nu-merical levels are difficult to compare due to scale differences, and the recurring bias must be extracted from prediction errors contaminated by random fluctuation. To address these challenges, we propose GRAM, a general retrieval-augmented framework for frozen TSFMs. GRAM first introduces an Amplitude Memory Module (AMM) that scales prediction errors by amplitude and aggregates them into retrievable prototypes. It then employs a Prototype Graph Module (PGM) to model relations among prototypes to aggregate consistent bias information while suppressing random fluctuation. During online forecasting, GRAM retrieves and expands prototypes relevant to the current query and generates per-horizon correc-tions to refine the original TSFM forecast. Experiments across multiple datasets and foundation models demonstrate consistent forecasting improvements. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) are pretrained on large-scale cross-domain corpora Shi et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2024c); Goswami et al. (2024); Ekambaram et al. (2024); Rasul et al. (2023); Garza & Canseco (2023); Gruver et al. (2023); Jin et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2024a); Liang et al. (2024); Wen et al. (2022), enabling a single model to transfer to diverse target scenarios for zero-shot forecasting without training a separate predictor for each dataset. This capability is particularly valuable when target-domain data are limited, model updates are costly, or the deployed predictor must remain frozen. However, despite their strong generalization ability, a pretrained TSFM may still exhibit systematic forecasting errors when transferred to a specific target domain Guo et al. (2017); Kim et al. (2022); Yao et al. (2022); Glahn & Lowry (1972). Effectively correcting TSFM prediction errors and improving forecasting accuracy while keeping the model parameters frozen is therefore of significant research and practical importance. Recent retrieval-augmented forecasting methods alleviate this problem by exploiting historical data from the target domain Yang et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2026). These methods retrieve historical 1arXiv:2610.04827v1 [cs.LG] 4 Oct 2026 Preprint windows with similar temporal patterns Agrawal & Srikant (1995); Mueen et al. (2009) and use the ground-truth future trajectories that follow them as auxiliary evidence. Their implicit assumption is that similar historical patterns correspond to transferable future trajectories. After removing the model’s prediction, the residual retains only the model’s forecasting error, di-rectly corresponding to the correction objective. However, a single residual is still not an ideal correction signal: the presence of ϵi means that directly reusing the residual of one historical win-dow may transfer random fluctuation along with the bias. Therefore, it is necessary to aggregate residual evidence from multiple related historical windows to extract the recurring model bias bi.However, this process faces two challenges. First, absolute residuals are strongly affected by the local numerical level of the series. Two historical windows may exhibit the same relative predic-tion bias, yet produce absolute residuals of vastly different magnitudes due to different numerical levels. Standard normalization can only align the global scale of a series and cannot eliminate local numerical differences between windows. Therefore, absolute residuals cannot be directly compared across windows with different numerical levels, and a scale-invariant bias representation is required. Second, the residual of a single historical window contains random fluctuation, making it difficult to reliably identify recurring model bias. Moreover, model bias and historical patterns are not in one-to-one correspondence: different historical patterns may produce similar model bias, and simi-lar historical patterns may also produce different model bias. Useful bias information may therefore be dispersed across multiple distinct but related historical windows, requiring explicit modeling of the relationships among windows to extract recurring model bias. To address the above problems, we propose G RAM , a retrieval-augmented forecasting framework for frozen TSFMs. G RAM constructs a structured bias memory and uses model bias to correct the current forecast, comprising two phases: Offline Memory Construction and Online Forecasting. In the Offline Memory Construction phase, the Amplitude Memory Module (AMM) represents model bias using amplitude scaling error rather than absolute residuals, making bias comparable across historical windows with different numerical levels, and aggregates historical windows into retriev-able prototypes. The Prototype Graph Module (PGM) establishes relationships among prototypes, organizing bias information dispersed across different historical windows into a unified structure. In the Online Forecasting phase, G RAM retrieves prototypes relevant to the current query and expands along the prototype graph to obtain broader bias information. The Graph Fusion Module (GFM) aggregates the retrieved bias information into per-horizon corrections, which are added to the origi-nal forecast of the frozen TSFM. Throughout the entire process, the TSFM and the retrieval encoder remain frozen. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose retrieving the prediction residuals of the TSFM on historical windows, so that retrieval retains only the information directly relevant to model correction. 2Preprint • We design the Amplitude Memory Module (AMM), which uses amplitude scaling error to char-acterize the prediction bias of the frozen TSFM under different historical patterns, and organizes related historical windows into retrievable prototypes. • We design the Prototype Graph Module (PGM), which establishes associations among prototypes to aggregate bias information dispersed across multiple historical windows. • We conduct extensive experiments on seven public benchmark datasets and four time-series foundation models. The results show that G RAM achieves the best forecasting performance across different datasets and foundation models. Additionally, the code is available at https: //github.com/ecnuabmlab/gram .

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 TIME -S ERIES FORECASTING WITH FOUNDATION MODELS 

Time-series forecasting has progressed from statistical models Box et al. (2015) to deep architectures that capture long-range, cross-variable, frequency or multi-scale patterns Flunkert et al. (2017); Lim et al. (2019); Oreshkin et al. (2020); Challu et al. (2023); Zhou et al. (2022); Liu et al. (2022a); Zhang & Yan (2023); Chen et al. (2023); all of these models are trained per dataset and depend on target labels. TSFMs instead pretrain once on large cross-domain corpora and forecast zero-shot on unseen domains, removing the dependence on target labels. Parameter-efficient adaptation further reduces the number of updated parameters but still requires target-specific optimization. In contrast, G RAM 

keeps the TSFM frozen and corrects its systematic scaling errors through an external memory. 2.2 RETRIEVAL -A UGMENTED FORECASTING 

Retrieval-augmented methods complement parametric models with non-parametric information se-lected from an external corpus Johnson et al. (2021). The principle originates in language model-ing Lewis et al. (2020); Guu et al. (2020); Izacard et al. (2022); Karpukhin et al. (2020); Khandelwal et al. (2020); Borgeaud et al. (2022); Gao et al. (2023) and has recently been extended to time-series forecasting. Existing methods differ mainly in how the retrieved evidence is incorporated. TimeRAG Yang et al. (2024) builds a knowledge base of time-series segments and couples retrieval with a large language model; TimeRAF Zhang et al. (2025) trains a bi-encoder that retrieves histor-ical windows and uses their futures as auxiliary signals for the forecast; TS-RAG Ning et al. (2025) augments a frozen TSFM with a retrieval knowledge base and fuses the retrieved patterns with the TSFM representation through an attention-based mixer; related designs adopt similar retrieve-then-fuse pipelines Han et al. (2025). These methods demonstrate that target-domain history can improve a frozen forecaster, but they generally treat retrieved windows as independent items and use similar-ity as the primary relevance signal. G RAM instead retrieves the scaling error that the frozen TSFM leaves on a pattern and reads it through a prototype graph: prototypes are connected by bias and shape relations, and the retrieved subgraph is processed by a trainable relational message-passing head whose output is gated by the evidence behind each retrieved prototype.

The offline phase distills the target history into two artifacts: the amplitude memory, which records how the TSFM errs on each pattern, and the prototype graph, which records which patterns err alike. Amplitudes rather than absolute residuals keep the memory dimensionless and reusable across corpora, and links between patterns that err alike let evidence recorded on one pattern be reused on another. The prototype graph connects the C prototypes with two relations, each prototype keeping its strongest neighbors under each relation; the out-degree is a hyper-parameter reported in the experi-ments. For the bias relation , we subtract one from each amplitude vector, forming the scaling-error curve ⃗α c − 1, and ℓ2-normalize it; the bias similarity is the cosine of these curves, and a bias edge requires the neighbor’s support to exceed a floor nmin , without which low-support prototypes could be linked by noise. For the shape relation , the similarity is the cosine of the centroids ¯ec; since a prototype with very small support has an amplitude close to one, because the weighted median over few members shrinks toward one, its shape neighbors provide the usable evidence for the read-out. The bias relation states that two prototypes are mis-scaled in the same way, which is the property that allows an amplitude correction to transfer between them. The graph fusion module maps the subgraph to the per-horizon error component ˆbq through three blocks, GNN, MHA and Gate, as shown in module E of Figure 1. It is fitted once on a large multi-domain corpus by minimizing the error of the corrected forecast, under the same frozen-TSFM contract: only the head is trained, the TSFM and the retrieval encoder remain frozen, and no label of the evaluation domains is used (Appendix A.4). Each node is featurized with its stored curves and scalar statistics, and a relational message-passing network with a separate projection per relation propagates them over the two relations (Appendix A.4); the anchor is excluded from message passing because it carries no edges. The query is encoded from scale statistics of its own excursion (Appendix A.4); a multi-head attention layer Vaswani et al. (2017) takes this vector as the only query and the propagated node representations as keys and values, and returns an attention-pooled vector together with the attention weights πq , one per subgraph node. A two-layer head maps the pooled and query features to a per-lead gain g(h) ≥ 0 that weights the voted scaling, and its last linear layer is initialized so that g ≡ 1 at the start of training: the head begins by passing the vote through unweighted and learns only the per-lead departures from it.

4 EXPERIMENTS 

Datasets. We evaluate on seven widely used benchmarks: ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, and ETTm2 Zhou et al. (2021), together with Weather, Electricity, and Exchange Rate Wu et al. (2023), following their canonical chronological splits. Dataset statistics and the exact split boundaries are reported in Appendix A.1. 

TSFMs. GRAM is attached to four TSFMs with different architectures and pretraining corpora: Chronos-Bolt Ansari et al. (2024), Sundial Liu et al. (2025), TimesFM Das et al. (2024), and Moirai Woo et al. (2024). All TSFMs remain frozen, and retrieval embeddings are computed by a frozen Chronos encoder shared across TSFMs, so a single correction pipeline is applied unchanged to every model. 

Setup. The context length is fixed to 512, and the forecast horizon is H ∈ { 96 , 192 , 336 , 720 }.Each channel is forecast independently. For each dataset–horizon pair and each TSFM, the official training split is divided chronologically into a memory portion and a calibration portion, separated from the test split by a horizon-length gap; the Amplitude Memory Module (AMM) and the Pro-totype Graph Module (PGM) are built only from these observed portions, and the Graph Fusion Module (GFM) is fitted once on the LOTSA corpus Woo et al. (2024) and then frozen for all target benchmarks and TSFMs. Only the GFM is trained; the TSFM and the retrieval encoder stay frozen, and no label of the evaluation domains is used. Since the only fitted part is fixed on an unrelated corpus before the test portion of a target cell is read, every comparison reported below is zero-shot with respect to the evaluation domains. The complete protocol, with the hyper-parameters and the machine used, is reported in Appendix A.1. 

Baselines and metrics. The baseline is the frozen TSFM itself: for each of the four models we compare its zero-shot forecast with the same forecast corrected by G RAM , on the same dataset and horizon, so that every comparison below is a like-for-like one between Ori and +GRAM . We also report LoRA fine-tuned versions of the four TSFMs Hu et al. (2022), which show whether the correction survives once the model has been adapted to the target domain. To ensure consistency with previous studies, we adopt Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) as evaluation metrics, always comparing MSE with MSE and MAE with MAE; lower is better in both. Reliability of the correction. The gate and the two fixed weights of Eq. equation 11 trade peak gain for coverage: the same correction without the graph, applying the pooled retrieval answer alone, improves both metrics in only 74 of the 112 settings and can lose up to 8.4% of MSE, whereas the deployed configuration improves both metrics in 107 of the 112 settings. Diagnostics of the pattern-conditioned bias. Figure 2 examines the bias over all 112 settings and yields three insights. First, the bias is real and pattern-conditioned: the frozen TSFMs systematically overstate how far the series departs from its recent level, and that overstatement varies about three times more inside a prototype than between prototypes, so the nearest pattern does not determine the bias of a window and the memory has to aggregate related prototypes. Second, the retrieved evidence is useful: the alignment between the correction it implies and the realised error beats a random-pairing baseline in all 112 settings and predicts the per-setting MSE gain of Table 1 with a correlation of 0.93 . Third, the evidence is available almost everywhere, with 53% to 82% of windows positively aligned depending on dataset and TSFM. 5 CONCLUSION 

GRAM corrects the forecasts of a frozen time-series foundation model by retrieving, from the target history, the amplitude of the error that the model itself leaves on recurring patterns, which keeps the evidence dimensionless and comparable across windows. Across seven benchmarks, four horizons and four heterogeneous frozen models it improves both metrics on every dataset under all four TSFMs, reaching reductions of 34.5% in MSE and 16.7% in MAE on the weakest model at the longest horizon, and it adds only 7.7 to 12.2 microseconds per window on top of the frozen pipeline. Two directions follow naturally: updating the memory online as new windows arrive, and extending the bias retrieval to multivariate settings.

A EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

A.1 DATASETS AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Table 4 lists the seven benchmarks and their split proportions. All of them are used exactly as published, with their canonical chronological splits Lai et al. (2018); Wu et al. (2021); Nie et al. (2023); Zeng et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2024b); Wang et al. (2024); Qiu et al. (2024); Godahewa et al. (2021); Aksu et al. (2024); Alexandrov et al. (2020), and both the memory-construction and the calibration portions are cut from the training portion, separated from each other and from the published test portion by a gap equal to the forecast horizon, so that no future observation of one portion can enter a window of the next; the test portion is never touched before evaluation. Aseparate memory is built for every dataset, horizon and TSFM, while the retrieval encoder and the graph fusion module are shared across all of them, and each channel is forecast independently. A.2 METRICS 

We report mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE), the two metrics used by previous studies on these benchmarks. Both are computed per channel against the un-normalised ground truth and then averaged over channels, and results are reported per dataset and horizon; when a table reports a single number for a dataset, as Table 1 and Table 7 do, it is the mean of the four horizon values of that dataset. A.3 MEMORY CONSTRUCTION AND READ -OUT SETTINGS 

The offline phase has no optimisation: one pass over the memory-construction portion of a target cell writes the record of Section 3.2.2, whose fields are the ones Eq. equation 2 to Eq. equation 4 define. Its principal settings are C = 128 prototypes, a recent level of S = 24 observations, a member weight of |exc |, a support floor of 8 for bias edges and L = 2 neighbours per relation. Online, the 

K = 3 closest prototypes seed a subgraph of about 21 nodes, each expanded by three neighbours per relation. The read-out has width 96 , two message-passing layers, four attention heads and dropout 

0.2, and enters the correction through the anchor weight μ = 0 .35 and the residual weight ν = 0 .15 .The read-out is fitted once, for 1500 steps with AdamW and a cosine schedule and three seeds, on the LOTSA corpus Woo et al. (2024), a multi-domain pretraining corpus unrelated to the seven evaluation benchmarks, and is then frozen; no series, forecast or label of an evaluation dataset takes part in that fitting, the frozen TSFM and the retrieval encoder are never updated, and the memory of a target cell is built only from the observed training portion of that cell. G RAM is therefore a zero-shot method on the evaluation benchmarks: zero-shot throughout this paper means that the frozen TSFM is used as published, the correction is fitted on an unrelated corpus, and no evaluation-domain data of any kind enters training. All measurements are taken on a single H200 with batches of 4096 

windows, and Table 8 reports the resulting cost of 7.7–12 .2 microseconds per window. A.4 RETRIEVAL AND THE GRAPH FUSION MODULE 

At inference the query embedding is compared with the C centroids and the subgraph of Sec-tion 3.3.1 is assembled from the seeds, the corpus prior and the anchor node: K = 3 seeds, each 15 Preprint expanded by three neighbours per relation, which yields 21 nodes on average. The record of a node is the five-tuple of Section 3.2.2, extended by the log support relative to the median prototype, the coherence, the retrieval similarity and the support flag; the query feature is the scale-normalised excursion of Eq. equation 1 followed by its log scale, its standard deviation and its maximum. The graph fusion module keeps the three blocks of Section 3.3.2 at width 96 , 4 attention heads and dropout 0.2 Srivastava et al. (2014). Eq. equation 8 is implemented as written: each term is an attention-weighted average over the subgraph, the four gains κi = softplus( ·) start at log 2 , the bias 

b starts at 2, and a subgraph whose nodes disagree about the scaling cannot be capped permissively. The deployed ratio is the one of Eq. equation 11, with the anchor weight μ = 0 .35 and the residual weight ν = 0 .15 ; the gain head’s last layer starts at zero so that g ≡ 1 and training begins from the fixed terms plus the vote, while the gate begins permissive and is only trained down where the evidence is thin. The head is fitted once on the LOTSA corpus Woo et al. (2024), whose series are split into memory and calibration portions with a fixed seed, and is then frozen for all target cells; the frozen TSFM and the retrieval encoder are never updated, and no label of an evaluation domain is used at any point. The fitting objective is the error of the corrected forecast—a squared and an absolute term—plus a penalty that keeps the returned amplitude close to the pooled retrieval answer. A.5 BASELINES AND FINE -TUNING PROTOCOL 

The baseline throughout is the frozen TSFM itself, whose zero-shot forecast is compared with the same forecast corrected by G RAM , on the same dataset and horizon and with the same metric. The parameter-efficient baselines are LoRA adapters optimised Kingma & Ba (2015) with each TSFM’s native training loss on chronological training windows; the adapter strength and the checkpoint are selected on a later chronological tail of the training split, and the selected model is frozen before the memory is built, so that the fine-tuned setting follows the same contract as the zero-shot one. A.6 PROTOCOL FOR THE DIAGNOSTICS 

The diagnostics of Figure 2 cover all 112 settings. To keep the pass cheap, each cell contributes at most 2000 test windows drawn uniformly over the test portion, and the forecast steps are thinned to 96 uniformly spaced leads before horizons are pooled on the relative axis h/H ; alignments, spreads and counts are computed on that thinned grid, and the amplitude of a window is its |exc |-weighted average. The random-pairing baseline permutes the retrieved evidence across test windows and averages five permutations. The quality of the retrieved evidence is measured by the cosine between the correction it implies and the realised error, which the main text calls its alignment. A.7 COMPUTE 

Building the memory is a single pass over the target history: O(n · C) comparisons for the assign-ment, then one pass per prototype to aggregate its members. A query costs one comparison against the centroids, the assembly of a 21-node subgraph, and the forward pass of the graph fusion mod-ule, whose parameters are three orders of magnitude below the frozen TSFM’s. The memory itself stores C records of H amplitude values plus five scalars, that is 128 × H floating point numbers per dataset, horizon and TSFM, which is negligible next to the frozen model.

B.5 EFFICIENCY AND MEMORY BUDGET 

The read-out is the only trained part and stays below a million parameters at every horizon, so the correction never dominates the cost of a backbone forward pass: the frozen backbones carry two to three orders of magnitude more parameters ( 128 M– 2B against 0.3–0.6M). What has to be stored per dataset, TSFM and horizon is the prototype memory— C = 128 records of H amplitudes and 

H spreads plus a handful of scalars, the centroid of each record being also the key it is retrieved by.
