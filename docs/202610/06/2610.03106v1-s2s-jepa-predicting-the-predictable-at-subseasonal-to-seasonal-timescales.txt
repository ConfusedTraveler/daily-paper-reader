S2S-JEPA: Predicting the Predictable at
Subseasonal-to-Seasonal Timescales

Abstract
The subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) timescale, roughly from two weeks to two months ahead, is a
critical forecast window for sectors such as agriculture, energy, and water management. Yet, it
is widely known as the ‘predictability desert’. Recent AI weather models excel up to two weeks
ahead but deteriorate beyond, largely because they are trained to predict fine-scale details
that are neither predictable nor essential at S2S timescales. We argue that a more physically
grounded objective is to forecast only the slowly varying components that remain predictable.
Computer vision reached the same conclusion with the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture
(JEPA), which predicts in latent space, discarding unpredictable details. In this work, we
introduce S2S-JEPA, which brings the JEPA paradigm to S2S forecasting. It is tailored to
this task through design elements from state-of-the-art AI weather models. S2S-JEPA achieves
comparable skill to the gold-standard ECMWF physics-based ensemble and surpasses it on
multiple metrics at weeks 5 to 6.
Keywords: AI weather model; S2S Prediction; JEPA
1 Introduction
Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) prediction, which aims to forecast weather conditions from roughly
two weeks to two months ahead, has long been a central challenge in atmospheric science. The
S2S timescale is a critical forecast window for decision-making in weather- and climate-sensitive
sectors, such as agriculture, energy, and water management [1]. It sits between medium-range
weather forecasting (up to two weeks ahead) and seasonal prediction, and it is widely known as the
‘predictability desert’ [2, 3]. S2S prediction is particularly challenging because it extends beyond
the timescale over which atmospheric initial conditions guide forecasts, yet remains too short for
slower components, such as sea surface temperature, soil moisture, and large-scale climate modes,
to fully dominate the forecast signal [4].

Traditional physics-based numerical prediction systems, built on the numerical integration of
the governing equations of atmospheric dynamics and their coupling with land, ocean, and sea-ice
components, form the backbone of operational S2S prediction [5]. These forecasts are physically
grounded, but integrating large coupled Earth-system models is computationally expensive. This is
particularly limiting at S2S lead times, where ensemble prediction is essential for isolating weak
but potentially predictable signals from internal atmospheric variability.
Meanwhile, data-driven models have rapidly advanced medium-range weather forecasting, with
many breakthroughs closely mirroring progress in artificial intelligence [6]. This architectural
transfer has been facilitated by the fact that most recent AI weather models are trained on gridded
reanalysis data, which casts forecasting as a dense spatiotemporal prediction task that resembles
computer-vision problems such as video prediction. Neural operators in FourCastNet [7], hierarchical
Transformers in Pangu-Weather and FuXi [8, 9], graph neural networks in GraphCast [10] and
diffusion models in GenCast [11] have successively been adapted to global atmospheric prediction.
More recently, foundation models such as ClimaX [12], Aurora [13] and Prithvi WxC [14] have
been pretrained on large-scale reanalysis and other meteorological datasets and then adapted to
downstream variables, resolutions, and lead times.
Despite their strong performance at medium-range lead times, these AI weather models often
struggle to extend their skill into the S2S range [15, 16]. A key limitation lies in their training
objective. By requiring the model to reconstruct the full atmospheric state on the grid, these
approaches devote capacity to details that become fundamentally unpredictable at longer lead times,
which drives forecasts beyond two weeks toward smoothed fields with little skill over climatology.
Data-driven approaches to S2S prediction have been explored from several directions [17–19].
One line of work treats the problem as statistical correction, learning to post-process dynamical
ensemble outputs or to predict targeted quantities from selected climate drivers [18, 20]. Other
studies adapt the modeling paradigm of medium-range forecasting to subseasonal horizons, as in
FuXi-S2S [19] and TianQuan-S2S [21], pretrain with masked reconstruction on reanalysis data, as
in Baguan [22], or generate future state sequences in the latent space of a variational autoencoder,
as in OmniCast [23]. In all of these models, however, the learning objective remains centered on
reproducing grid-level details of the atmospheric state, either directly or through a latent space
trained by reconstruction. We argue that an objective better aligned with the physics of the Earth
system is to forecast the slowly varying components that remain predictable at S2S timescales,
2


such as tropical intraseasonal oscillations, stratospheric variability, and persistent land and oceanic
anomalies, rather than fine-scale details that do not.
Remarkably, computer vision, the field that has repeatedly driven architectural innovation in AI
for weather research, reached the same conclusion from a different starting point. Masked prediction
has become a dominant paradigm in self-supervised learning, with models reconstructing missing
content from visible context [24, 25]. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) recast
this idea in representation space, where the model infers the representations of hidden targets
from context instead of reconstructing raw observations [26, 27]. The formulation has since been
applied to masked image regions [27], spatiotemporal video blocks [28, 29], and world models that
forecast future latent states for planning and control [30–32]. Imagine watching a tree swaying in
the wind [26]: you neither track the motion of every leaf nor could you predict it; instead, what you
perceive and anticipate is the higher-level structure of the scene—the tree continuing to sway with
the wind. JEPA builds on this intuition. Rather than predicting the raw signal in all its unpredictable
detail, it predicts a representation that captures what is predictable and sufficient for understanding
what happens next. This philosophy aligns well with the physical character of S2S predictability,
where skill resides in a few slow, large-scale modes rather than in the full atmospheric state.
Motivated by this correspondence, we reformulate S2S forecasting as latent-space prediction,
in which the model is tasked with forecasting the evolution of the Earth system’s predictable
structure. In this work, we present S2S-JEPA, a fully data-driven S2S model built on the joint-
embedding predictive principle. Recent work has begun to explore JEPA for regional atmospheric
prediction [33]. However, to our knowledge, S2S-JEPA is the first to extend this principle to global
subseasonal forecasting, learning a predictive representation of the Earth system weeks into the
future. S2S-JEPA adapts the encoder-predictor architecture of V-JEPA [28, 29] from video to
the Earth system, encoding daily snapshots of 79 atmospheric, land-surface and oceanic variables
relevant to S2S predictability in place of frames of RGB pixels. The training formulation, in contrast,
is tailored to the physics of S2S prediction. The model forecasts the future weekly latent states
of the Earth system, so that S2S-timescale predictability itself supervises the encoder and the
learned representation is forced to extract the slowly evolving signals that carry S2S predictability.
S2S-JEPA also adopts design choices that have proven effective at the medium range [8, 12, 13, 34],
as detailed in Section 2. It delivers forecast skill competitive with the ECMWF operational S2S
ensemble [5], the gold standard of subseasonal prediction, at weeks 5 to 6 and at a fraction of the
3


computational cost. The model trains and runs on a single GPU, whereas operational S2S forecasts
require ensemble integrations of a physics-based model on dedicated supercomputing infrastructure.
2 Method
2.1 Problem setup
We focus on the operational S2S forecasting task adapted from the ECMWF AI Weather Quest [35].
Given an initialization day t0, the task is to deliver probabilistic forecasts of weekly-mean 2-meter
temperature (T2M), weekly accumulated precipitation (TP), and weekly-mean sea-level pressure
(MSLP) for the following K = 6 weeks. We refer to these three weekly quantities as the forecast
products. Temperature and precipitation are evaluated over land and sea-level pressure globally, in
accordance with AI Weather Quest [35].
Our models operate on V = 79 atmospheric, land-surface and oceanic variables from the ERA5
reanalysis [36], regridded to 1.5◦(H × W = 120 × 240; see Tab. 4 for the full list). All variables are
expressed as standardized anomalies with respect to a day-of-year climatology computed over 1979–
2018 (see Appendix A for details). This formulation isolates the forecast problem from the known
seasonal cycle, focusing the model on the departures from climatology that constitute predictability
at S2S timescales.
Let Xt ∈RV ×H×W denote daily-mean anomaly fields of day t. We group consecutive days into
weeks of T = 7 days, indexed relative to the initialization day t0. Week k is defined as
X(k) = (Xt0+7k−6, . . . , Xt0+7k) ∈RT ×V ×H×W ,

2.2 Joint-embedding predictive formulation and two-stage training
Joint-embedding predictive architectures [26, 27] learn by predicting the representation of a hidden
part of a signal from that of a visible part. A critical challenge in joint-embedding learning is
representation collapse, where all inputs are mapped to the same or nearly identical embedding.
The target representations are therefore produced by a slowly evolving teacher, an exponential
moving average of the online encoder, which stabilizes training and helps prevent this trivial solution.
S2S-JEPA treats the past week of the Earth system as the context and the unseen future week as
the hidden target. Training proceeds in two stages, the first on one-step prediction of the following
week, the second on autoregressive rollouts across the full forecast range (Figure 1). Separating the
two stages allows the representation to be learned under a one-step objective before the predictor
is trained on its own outputs. The separation also proved necessary in practice, since training on
5


rollouts from the start destabilized the representation and led to its rapid collapse in our early
experiments.

gradient evaluated on the rollout distribution, the ‘pushforward trick’ that Aurora adopts for its
roll-out fine-tuning [13, 37].
2.3 Model architecture
The model components are drawn from the weather-modeling literature. Their description is
therefore kept brief, and the details are given in Appendix B. In the encoder, a grouped weather
embedding [12, 13] tokenizes the 79 variables into N = 4000 tokens on five latent levels, from the
ocean-land boundary through the near-surface state to the stratosphere, and a Swin Transformer
backbone [8, 38] processes them with windows spanning all levels. The predictor uses one learnable
query for each output token, with global cross-attention to the encoded state to account for distant
dependencies such as teleconnections, and windowed self-attention among the queries for local
information exchange. It outputs a full latent state in the same token space as the encoder, so
that prediction and target can be compared directly under the joint-embedding loss. Its only
conditioning is seasonality, an input that prior S2S work has found effective [21]. The climatology
and top-of-atmosphere insolation of the input week modulate every token through FiLM layers [39].
2.4 Deterministic and probabilistic readout
Since S2S-JEPA predicts latent states, the forecast products are obtained from them by two readout
modules. The deterministic readout provides a single estimate of the weekly anomalies, and the
probabilistic readout generates an ensemble of forecasts, from which the quintile probabilities
required by the task are computed. In both cases, no gradient reaches the encoder or the predictor,
so that the readouts do not influence the representation.
The deterministic readout is a lightweight convolutional decoder that maps each latent state to
the weekly-mean anomalies of all 79 variables. It is trained alongside the backbone on detached
latent states, which also allows it to monitor training. Its output serves as the deterministic forecast
in Sec. 3 and as the basis for model selection.
7


The probabilistic readout follows the approach of ArchesWeatherGen [40], which augments
a deterministic forecasting model with a diffusion-based generative model trained to sample the
residual distribution the deterministic forecast cannot express. One head per forecast product is
trained in the EDM framework [11, 41] on the standardized residual, conditioned on the deterministic
forecast, the predicted latent tokens through cross-attention, the lead time and the day of year
(see Appendix B). This diffusion-based generative model is trained separately, with the backbone
of S2S-JEPA frozen. At inference, 50 samples per target week form the ensemble, and quintile
probabilities are the fractions of members falling into each category.
2.5 Evaluation protocol
We evaluate against the ECMWF extended-range ensemble from the S2S database [5], using its
101-member real-time forecasts and the 11-member reforecasts of the preceding 20 years. The test
set consists of the 102 Wednesday initializations between December 2023 and November 2025 for
which both systems are available, and ERA5 weekly aggregates serve as ground truth for both.
Deterministic skill is measured by the temporal correlation coefficient (TCC) of anomalies at each
grid point, computed over all test initializations. We report TCC both as maps and as area-weighted
averages, taken over land for temperature and precipitation and globally for sea-level pressure.
Probabilistic skill is measured by the fair ranked probability skill score (fair RPSS) of quintile
forecasts, the metric of the ECMWF AI Weather Quest [35]. Quintile boundaries are derived for
each system from its own 20-year hindcast climate and for the observations from the ERA5 record
over the same years, following FuXi-S2S [19]; this removes model bias and drift from the comparison.
For 2-metre temperature, a linear trend fitted separately to the forecasts and to the observations is
removed before the boundaries are computed, so that the warming trend does not count as skill.
Unlike FuXi-S2S, we do not detrend mean sea-level pressure and precipitation in the main text, for
which the long-term trends are relatively weak.

3.3 Sources of forecast skill
We use controlled ablations to motivate the key design choices of S2S-JEPA, with all variants
evaluated on the same test set under the protocol of Sec. 2.5 using TCC averaged across the
three variables (Table 3). In particular, we examine the forecasting formulation, the role of latent
prediction, and the training strategy for autoregressive rollouts.
We first examine the choice of forecasting formulation for our S2S setting, as different formulations
have been explored for S2S prediction in AI weather models, including autoregressive forecasting [19]
and direct prediction at prescribed lead times [21]. In fact, we initially expected lead-conditioned
forecasting to be better suited to the S2S timescale, given the potential accumulation of errors over
repeated autoregressive steps and the strong performance demonstrated by TianQuan-S2S [21].
For a controlled comparison, we consider two direct forecasting variants with the same backbone:
a lead-conditioned model, with the target lead time (weeks 1–6) encoded and injected into each
Transformer block through adaptive LayerNorm conditioning following Stormer [34], and per-lead
experts trained separately for each target week [21]. Both variants use the same JEPA-style training
objective, with the loss applied in latent space, isolating the forecasting formulation as the main
difference. The results nevertheless favor autoregressive forecasting. The lead-conditioned model
performs substantially worse across all lead times, while the per-lead experts achieve slightly higher
skill at week 1 (0.620 versus 0.609) but fall behind from week 2 onward. We attribute these differences
in part to the opposite trade-offs during pretraining: lead conditioning requires a single model to
accommodate prediction tasks across widely different timescales, whereas per-lead experts restrict
each model to samples from a single target lead time, reducing the diversity of its pretraining data.
Autoregressive forecasting balances these two aspects by defining a consistent prediction task while
retaining the diversity of training samples across lead times.

Interestingly, the supervised model achieves higher skill at week 1 than S2S-JEPA (0.647 versus

single-week prediction. This suggests that, while learning in latent space does not improve one-step

The same pattern remains after rollout training (Stage 2). Both models improve from week 2 to
week 6 at the cost of a slight decrease at week 1, and S2S-JEPA retains substantially better skill

requires the model to account for the full target state, including variability that becomes increasingly
unpredictable at longer timescales. In contrast, JEPA shifts the prediction target to latent space,
encouraging representations that emphasize the more predictable components of the Earth system.
We believe this is what allows skill learned from one-step prediction to persist naturally toward
longer autoregressive leads.

We emphasize that this does not imply that direct field reconstruction is unsuitable for S2S
prediction, as previous studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in S2S forecasting [19, 21]. Rather,
the week-to-week setup considered here is particularly well aligned with the spirit of S2S-JEPA,
as the long input and target windows emphasize slowly evolving and more predictable modes,
while reconstructing the full target fields over the same interval makes direct supervised learning
substantially more challenging.
Finally, we examine the training strategy for the autoregressive model, comparing the rollout
training strategies explored during model development. Here, we report representative comparisons
among two-stage training, curriculum learning, and mixed-horizon rollout training. Two-stage
training [7, 13], which first learns one-step prediction and then fine-tunes on rollouts, performs best
at the S2S leads and is therefore adopted in the final S2S-JEPA (Stages 1–2 in Fig. 1). Curriculum
learning [10, 19], which progressively increases the rollout depth, shifts skill toward the earlier weeks
at the expense of longer leads. Mixed-horizon rollout training [34], which back-propagates through
the rollout and jointly optimizes multiple forecast steps, performs slightly worse overall, although
the gap becomes small at weeks 5–6. Overall, these results favor explicit one-step pretraining
followed by rollout fine-tuning for optimizing skill across the S2S range, leading us to adopt this
strategy in S2S-JEPA.
4 Discussion
In this work, we introduced S2S-JEPA, a latent-space forecasting framework for S2S prediction.
The framework is motivated by a fundamental distinction between S2S and medium-range weather
forecasting. As lead time increases, the precise evolution of individual weather systems becomes
increasingly unpredictable, while slower and larger-scale components of the Earth system can retain
useful predictability. S2S-JEPA therefore shifts the learning objective from reproducing every detail
of the future atmospheric state toward predicting what remains predictable, the same principle that
JEPA formalizes for world models by placing the prediction objective in a learned representation
space [26]. We hypothesized that it could translate into improved forecast skill. This hypothesis
follows a broader lesson from data-driven weather forecasting, where the successful adaptation
of ideas from computer vision has often relied on integrating architectural innovations with the
underlying physics as well as on transferring them [7, 8, 10, 13, 42].
14


In our formulation, S2S-JEPA first learns to predict the latent representation of the following
week from the preceding week, and is then trained on autoregressive rollouts to extend predic-
tion to six weeks. Probabilistic ensembles are generated by coupling the latent predictor with a
diffusion-based generative model. We evaluate both deterministic and probabilistic skill against the
operational ECMWF extended-range ensemble. Across both settings, S2S-JEPA becomes increas-
ingly competitive with ECMWF as lead time increases. For deterministic forecasts, S2S-JEPA is
significantly worse than the ECMWF ensemble mean at week 3, but by weeks 5 and 6 it matches it
for MSLP and TP and achieves significantly higher TCC for T2M. A similar transition emerges in
probabilistic forecasting. Despite a clear disadvantage across all three variables at week 3, S2S-JEPA
matches or exceeds ECMWF at longer lead times for T2M and TP, with significant improvements
for T2M at week 6 and TP at weeks 5 and 6, while ECMWF retains an advantage for MSLP.
This lead-time dependence is further supported by the ablation against supervised learning, where
the relative benefit of latent prediction likewise becomes more pronounced at longer rollout hori-
zons. Together, these observations are consistent with our hypothesis. We expect the benefits of
latent prediction to emerge only as the detailed evolution of the weather loses predictability while
large-scale structure persists.
Despite its competitive performance at longer lead times, S2S-JEPA remains substantially less
skillful than ECMWF at weeks 1–3, constituting an important limitation of the current framework.
It is therefore also expected to be less competitive at shorter lead times than daily-resolution
models such as FuXi-S2S [19], whose short-lead skill is comparable to the ECMWF ensemble.
This short-lead deficit may reflect a trade-off inherent in our formulation, where by emphasizing
predictable information in latent space, together with the weekly temporal aggregation of both
inputs and targets, the model may discard fine-grained information that remains valuable when
the evolution of individual weather systems is still relatively predictable. Understanding whether
this trade-off is intrinsic to our latent prediction formulation, and whether short-lead skill can be
improved through alternative task formulations without sacrificing longer-lead performance, is an
important direction for future work. We also acknowledge that S2S-JEPA does not outperform
ECMWF across all variables and metrics even at longer lead times. One possible explanation is that
the latent prediction objective selectively preserves predictable information, but the information
retained in the learned representation may not align equally well with every variable. This issue
may be less pronounced in video, where the input channels typically represent RGB values and are
jointly interpreted as visual content. At S2S timescales, by contrast, different physical variables
15


can have substantially different levels and sources of predictability, potentially biasing the learned
representation toward a certain subset of variables.
More broadly, as the first exploration of JEPA for S2S prediction, S2S-JEPA demonstrates the
feasibility of this direction while leaving substantial room for further development. One natural
extension is to scale pretraining to larger and more diverse Earth-system datasets, following the
scaling trend already established in Earth-system foundation models [12, 13]. Another promising
direction is to incorporate masking strategies commonly used in JEPA pretraining. In particular,
decades of S2S research have provided substantial prior knowledge about sources of subseasonal
predictability, opening the possibility of physics-aware masking that selectively exposes or masks
known sources of predictability. Such a strategy could encourage the model to learn physically
meaningful predictive relationships while preventing reliance on simple shortcuts. Meanwhile,
explainability is increasingly important in data-driven weather and climate prediction, not only
for building trust in forecasts but also for advancing scientific understanding [43, 44]. Interpreting
the learned representations with emerging XAI tools could reveal the physical drivers behind S2S-
JEPA’s predictions and offer new insights into the intrinsic predictability of the Earth system [45].
Finally, rapidly evolving JEPA formulations, such as LeWorldModel [31], together with growing
applications of JEPA across scientific domains, offer alternative approaches to learning latent
dynamics and could provide useful building blocks for future S2S-JEPA models. Together, these
directions highlight representation-space prediction as a promising new avenue for extended-range
Earth-system forecasting [46].

A.2 Data Preprocessing
All inputs are ERA5 [36] daily fields for 1979–2025 on a 1.5◦grid conservatively regridded from
0.25◦; accumulated quantities (precipitation and top net thermal radiation) as daily totals, all others
as daily means. To account for the heavy-tailed distribution of precipitation, daily precipitation is
transformed as log(1 + x/ε), where ε is the 10th percentile of positive daily totals in the training
years (1.3×10−5 m). S2S-JEPA operates in anomaly space, with anomalies defined relative to a fixed
day-of-year climatology computed exclusively from the training years 1979–2018. Note that this
training climatology is distinct from the 2004–2023 verification climatology used to compute TCC.
For each variable and grid point, the climatological mean and standard deviation are estimated by
pooling samples at day offsets −4, −2, 0, 2, 4 around each calendar day and smoothing the resulting
day-of-year statistics using the first four annual harmonics. Anomalies are then computed as
(x−µdoy)/σdoy and clipped at ±10 as a safeguard against extreme outliers. The −90◦row (zero area
weight) is dropped, giving a 120 × 240 grid on which all forecasts and verification are carried out.
B Model Architecture Detail
B.1 S2S-JEPA
Encoder.
The encoder maps the seven context days to a latent state through grouped embedding followed by
a stack of Swin Transformer blocks. Inspired by the variable-wise embedding of ClimaX [12] and
the latent-level aggregation of Aurora [13], our grouped embedding partitions the 79 variables into
16 physically motivated groups, which are further organized into five latent levels (Tab. 5). For each
group, the seven context days are stacked along the channel dimension, following the treatment of
consecutive states in GraphCast and FuXi [9, 10], and embedded by a group-specific convolution
with 6 × 6 patches. Ocean and land variables form a boundary level; near-surface state and column
variables form a surface level; and pressure-level variables are divided into lower-tropospheric (1000–
700 hPa), free-tropospheric (600–200 hPa), and stratospheric (100–10 hPa) levels, reflecting the
distinct physical regimes and the broad physical structure of the Earth system. Within each latent
level, the resulting group embeddings are concatenated and projected to a single token per patch,
to which sinusoidal level and positional embeddings are added. This yields N = 5 × 20 × 40 = 4000

The predictor maps the encoded state to the latent representation of the following week. It first
applies two global self-attention blocks to globally contextualize the encoded state, which then
serves as the memory for prediction. To construct the next latent state, one learned query is assigned
to each of the N = 4000 positions on the 5 × 20 × 40 latent grid. These queries are processed by
eight predictor blocks, each consisting of global cross-attention to the encoded memory, windowed
self-attention among the queries, and an MLP. Global cross-attention allows each predicted token
to access the entire current state, capturing long-range dependencies such as teleconnections,
while windowed self-attention enables local information exchange among tokens of the predicted
state. Unlike predictors conditioned on lead time [34], ours always advances the state by one
week and is instead conditioned on the input week climatology. The climatological mean and log-
standard deviation of all variables at the input week, together with top-of-atmosphere insolation,
are embedded following the same level structure as the encoder and modulate the three branches of
every query block through FiLM layers [39]. The predictor thus defines a non-autonomous operator
with explicit seasonal forcing. It predicts all five latent levels, including the boundary level, yielding
24


a complete latent state that can be fed directly into the next prediction step. During training, each
step is trained against the teacher representation of its corresponding target week.
B.2 Deterministic readout head
A lightweight convolutional head decodes the latent state into physical fields and is shared across
all rollout steps. At each spatial patch, the tokens from the five latent levels are concatenated and
passed through a 1 × 1 convolution, a 3 × 3 convolution, and a final 1 × 1 convolution with GELU
activations. The output channels are then rearranged into 6 × 6 pixels by pixel shuffle, recovering
the 120 × 240 grid. The head predicts standardized weekly-mean anomalies for all 79 variables,
rather than only the three forecast targets, providing a diagnostic of how much information about
the full Earth-system state is retained in the latent representation. Precipitation is instead predicted
as the weekly total in the logarithmic space, since averaging daily values after the log transform
does not preserve the weekly total.
The readout head is trained jointly with the backbone but acts only as a probe: its latitude-
weighted mean-squared-error loss is computed against the ground truth, and gradients are stopped
at the latent state so that the readout loss does not shape the learned representation. At inference,
the shared head is applied after each of the six rollout steps, producing deterministic forecasts at
7–42-day leads for evaluation in Section 2.5.
B.3 Diffusion-based generative model
Ensemble members are generated by a conditional diffusion model that learns the residual between
the verifying truth and the deterministic forecast, with both the backbone and readout head kept
frozen. A separate model is trained for each of the three target variables.
The diffusion model is conditioned on the decoded deterministic forecasts of the three target
variables at the same lead, on the latent state from which they were decoded, on the forecast lead,
and on the day of year of the target week. The latent state is the predictor output at that lead: one
vector per 6 × 6 patch, formed by concatenating the five level tokens of the patch (5 × 512 channels),
which gives 800 latent tokens on the 20 × 40 patch grid. The denoising network is a U-Net on the
120 × 240 grid whose two coarsest stages work on the same 20 × 40 grid, so that every feature of
these stages corresponds to exactly one latent token. At these stages each field token attends to all
800 latent tokens through cross-attention. The diffusion-based generator therefore reads the latent
state that produced the forecast, not only the decoded fields. Following GenCast [11], we adopt the
25


EDM preconditioning and training objective [41] with unit data scale. Each model is trained on
1979–2018, and the checkpoint with the lowest fair CRPS on 2019–2021 is selected.
Ensemble members are sampled using the second-order Heun solver of EDM with 20 steps along
a power-law noise schedule. The resulting ensemble is calibrated separately for each variable and
lead on initializations from January 2022 to 6 December 2023, preceding the test period. Members
are centred on the deterministic forecast and their deviations are scaled, in the standardised frame
and for precipitation in the logarithmic frame, such that the ensemble spread matches the error
of the deterministic forecast. Calibration is performed in standardized anomaly space and, for
precipitation, in logarithmic space, before applying the inverse transformations. The test ensembles
contain 50 members, while the hindcasts used to construct the model’s own climatology (Sec. 2.5)
contain 11 members.
C Training details
All models were trained on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU with PyTorch 2.7, bfloat16 autocast and
the AdamW optimiser (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, weight decay 0.05 for the forecast model and 0.1 for
the diffusion heads). Gradients are clipped to a global norm of 1. Learning rates follow a warmup–
stable–decay schedule: a linear warmup, a constant plateau and a linear decay to zero over the final
iterations given in Table 6. Snapshots are written every 1,000 iterations and the reported model is
the snapshot with the best validation skill, never the last one.
Table 6 lists the detailed settings of the two training stages. Stage 1 trains the encoder, the
predictor and the readout head on (one step) with the joint-embedding objective: the predicted
latent state is compared with the LayerNorm-standardised, stop-gradient encoding of the target
week by an exponential-moving-average (EMA) teacher. The loss is the L1 distance per token,
weighted by the cosine of latitude; each latent dimension is rescaled by the inverse of its running
standard deviation so that no dimension dominates. The teacher momentum increases linearly from
0.999 to 1 over the stage. Stage 2 continues from the Stage-1 weights at iteration 10,000 with a
fresh optimiser and rolls the predictor for all K = 6 steps. Every step is trained against the teacher
encoding of its own target week with the same objective, the latent state is detached between steps,
and the teacher is frozen. The learning rate of the backbone is 25 times smaller than in Stage 1; the
readout head keeps its learning rate. Stage 2 also switches the precipitation target of the readout
head to the weekly total in the logarithmic frame. The validation set is a fixed subset of 1,048
initialisations from 2019–2021 on which the model is rolled out to 42 days every 500 iterations; the
26


Stage-2 snapshot at iteration 2,000 had the best validation skill at weeks 3–6 and is the model used
throughout the paper.

D Significance Test
Differences between two systems are assessed with a paired moving-block bootstrap over the
102 initialisation dates. Weekly initialisations are not independent, because target weeks overlap
and consecutive initialisations share the same slowly varying background, so resampling single
dates would overstate the effective sample size. We therefore resample blocks of eight consecutive
27


initialisations with replacement until 102 dates are reached, and use the same resampled dates
for both systems, so that the difference is evaluated on identical weather. For the TCC the per-
grid-point correlation is recomputed on each resample before the area-weighted mean is taken; for
the RPSS the per-initialisation area-mean RPS of both systems and of the uniform forecast are
resampled and the skill scores recomputed, with climatologies, trends and calibration kept fixed.
Each test uses 1,000 resamples; a difference is called significant when the 2.5–97.5 percentile interval
of its bootstrap distribution excludes zero. Blocks of eight weeks absorb sub-seasonal autocorrelation
but not interannual variability; with two test years the intervals should be read as optimistic.
E Trend removal in the probabilistic evaluation
Following the protocol of FuXi-S2S, a linear trend can be removed from forecasts and observations
before the quintile categories are formed, so that no skill is awarded for reproducing the warming
of the reference period. The main text applies this step to T2M only, because its trend is large and
well estimated, whereas the MSLP trend is negligible and the per-grid-point precipitation trend
is dominated by sampling noise. Figure 4 shows the same evaluation with the trend removed for
MSLP and TP as well. Here, the scores shift in favour of S2S-JEPA, mainly because the ECMWF
scores decrease markedly; we attribute this to the noise of the extrapolated precipitation trend
rather than to forecast skill and therefore do not use it in the main text.
