Abstract 

For a given point forecaster, proportional–integral–derivative (PID) calibration con-figurations can attain similar overall coverage yet produce different interval widths. We introduce PID-Expert, an online aggregation method for improving the efficiency of con-formal prediction intervals for time series. PID-Expert combines thresholds from a fixed library of PID calibrators, each evolving under its own coverage feedback. Aggregation weights depend on normalized interval width and miscoverage, while a shared multiplier adapts the miscoverage penalty using feedback from the reported interval. We establish local regret bounds for weighted expert losses under the realized multiplier sequence and, separately, a pathwise upper bound on time-averaged aggregate miscoverage, with control in expectation and almost surely under a stability condition. Across four simulation set-tings and two real-data applications, PID-Expert produces narrower mean intervals than a prespecified Conformal PID benchmark while keeping overall empirical coverage close to the nominal level. Compared with expert selection and equal averaging, aggregation gener-ally provides a more balanced coverage–width trade-off across forecasting settings. Rolling analyses further reveal local coverage–efficiency trade-offs, particularly following abrupt distributional shifts. Overall, PID-Expert reduces reliance on a single PID configuration while improving interval efficiency. 

Keywords: Online conformal prediction, Adaptive calibration, Expert aggregation, Prediction interval efficiency, Time series forecasting 

1. Introduction 

Online prediction intervals need to keep miscoverage near a target rate while avoiding unnecessary width. This balance between calibration and sharpness is central to probabilis-tic forecasting (Gneiting et al., 2007; Gneiting and Katzfuss, 2014) and interval forecasting practice (Chatfield, 1993; Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, 2021; Petropoulos et al., 2022).

For a given point forecaster, calibration rules with similar overall coverage can still produce intervals of different widths. Online conformal methods adjust calibration through realized coverage errors as new observations arrive (Gibbs and Candès, 2021; Angelopoulos et al., 2023). Even when these adjustments bring overall coverage close to the target, the choice of calibration rule can leave room to improve interval efficiency. Conformal proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control makes this choice concrete (Angelopoulos et al., 2023). It updates score thresholds through recent coverage errors, accumulated coverage deviations, and forecasts of future nonconformity scores. The pro-portional gain, integral gain, and saturation constant determine how these feedback sig-nals affect the threshold path. Faster responses can produce more variable intervals, while slower or more conservative responses can require wider intervals. Coverage feedback ad-justs an individual controller but does not directly compare its interval efficiency with that of other configurations. Thus, even with a PID calibrator in place, a practical choice remains: which feedback configuration avoids unnecessary width while keeping coverage close to the target? To assess how much interval efficiency depends on this choice, we compare PID con-figurations under a common forecasting setup. In the nonlinear simulation in Section 5, 343 PID controllers share the same point forecasts, nonconformity scores, and scorecaster (Figure 4). Their empirical coverage ranges from 0.901 to 0.909 at a target of 0.9, yet their mean interval widths differ. The prespecified Conformal PID controller has mean width 

0.360 , compared with 0.352 for PID-Expert and 0.348 for the narrowest library member selected retrospectively among those with empirical coverage of at least 0.9. These differ-ences motivate combining PID configurations online, using their observed performance to guide the construction of prediction intervals. The closest existing approaches address related choices at different stages of forecast-ing and calibration. Adaptive conformal inference (ACI), introduced by Gibbs and Candès (2021), adjusts the calibration level in response to observed coverage errors. AgACI, as originally proposed by Zaffran et al. (2022), aggregates interval bounds from ACI pre-dictors with different step sizes using Bernstein online aggregation (Wintenberger, 2017), while DtACI (Gibbs and Candès, 2024) adapts their influence through their calibration levels. These methods reduce reliance on a single ACI learning rate. Forecast combina-tions address uncertainty at the predictive-model stage (Petropoulos et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023). Model-selection approaches also adapt across model-specific conformal pre-dictors (Hajihashemi and Shen, 2024), and Bellman conformal inference optimizes interval length using families of nominal prediction intervals (Yang et al., 2024). Our focus is the efficiency left by PID calibration configurations for a shared point forecaster. We combine the threshold paths generated by distinct feedback histories and use coverage of the final reported interval to adjust how these paths are evaluated. We propose PID-Expert aggregation with two connected feedback layers. At the expert layer, each PID controller maintains its own coverage history and generates a candidate threshold. Keeping these states separate preserves the different calibration paths available 2for comparison. At the aggregation layer, weights combine the candidate thresholds and are updated using normalized interval width and expert miscoverage. Coverage of the reported interval updates a common multiplier that sets the relative penalty on miscoverage in these expert losses. This additional feedback is needed because coverage of an averaged threshold is not the weighted average of the experts’ coverage indicators. The multiplier therefore brings the reported interval’s coverage into the comparison of calibration paths. The paper makes three contributions. First, PID-Expert aggregation turns reliance on one prespecified PID configuration into an online comparison of independently evolving calibration paths, with an explicit width–miscoverage loss informed by aggregate coverage. Second, the analysis separates the two questions created by this design: local regret bounds compare weighted expert losses with a fixed library member under the realized multiplier sequence, while a separate pathwise relation links aggregate miscoverage to multiplier growth. A stability condition then gives time-averaged upper control in expectation and almost surely; the regret bound also motivates reference aggregation parameters. Third, four simulation settings and two real-data applications evaluate PID-Expert aggregation against the prespecified Conformal PID benchmark, DtACI, and AgACI, together with expert selection and simple averaging as structured benchmarks for isolating the role of online aggregation. Across the reported forecasting configurations, PID-Expert aggregation produces narrower mean intervals than the prespecified PID benchmark while maintaining empirical coverage close to the target. The comparison with selection and averaging shows that the gains are not explained simply by choosing a favorable expert or averaging expert outputs, while rolling-window analyses further characterize the associated local coverage– efficiency tradeoffs. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant liter-ature and introduces the online prediction setup and conformal PID calibration. Section 3 presents PID-Expert aggregation. Section 4 develops the regret and coverage analysis. Section 5 evaluates the method through simulations and real-data applications. Section 6 discusses the findings and concludes. Proofs and additional implementation details are provided in the appendices. 

2. Background and Problem Setup 

This section places the calibration problem in the time-series literature, defines the online prediction setting, and introduces the PID states used by the expert library. 

2.1. Conformal prediction for time series 

Conformal prediction constructs prediction sets by calibrating nonconformity scores, which measure disagreement between predictions and observations (Vovk et al., 2005; Shafer and Vovk, 2008). Split conformal prediction separates model fitting from calibra-tion and selects a score quantile from a calibration sample, obtaining finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability (Papadopoulos et al., 2002; Lei et al., 2018; Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023). 3Temporal dependence and structural change motivate approaches beyond exchangeable calibration. In the forecasting literature, Koo and Seo (2015) study structural-break mod-els under misspecification and their implications for forecasting, while Koo et al. (2026) distinguish changes in factor variances from changes in loadings in macroeconomic fac-tor models. In conformal prediction, weighted calibration addresses covariate shift and other departures from exchangeability (Tibshirani et al., 2019; Barber et al., 2023), while permutation-based methods account for dependent data (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). Xu and Xie (2021) combine bootstrap ensembles with sequential residual calibration, and Xu and Xie (2023) use temporal dependence in scores to estimate conditional score quan-tiles. Their guarantees depend on the respective assumptions about the score process and estimation accuracy. Other extensions broaden the prediction target. Conformal methods have been devel-oped for multiple forecast horizons (Stankeviciute et al., 2021; Wang and Hyndman, 2024), multistep multivariate prediction (Schlembach et al., 2025), and functional time series with applications to age-specific mortality (Shang, 2026). These studies extend calibration to prediction tasks with different temporal and response structures. Online feedback provides a further approach: ACI uses realized coverage errors to update subsequent calibration levels (Gibbs and Candès, 2021). Building on ACI, Zaffran et al. (2022) aggregate intervals from predictors with different step sizes, while Gibbs and Candès (2024) dynamically adapt the choice of step size. Such updates can also exploit predictable structure. Sun and Yu (2025) combine latent-state prediction with adaptive calibration to address change points. Areces et al. (2025) formulate online conformal prediction through online optimization, and Hu et al. (2026) incorporate estimated score distributions into optimistic threshold updates. We study one-step symmetric intervals, holding the point forecasts and score information fixed to compare the efficiency of different PID calibration paths.

3.1. Overview 

Our objective is to achieve a favorable coverage–efficiency tradeoff under evolving data distributions by constructing narrow prediction intervals while maintaining coverage close to the target level. To this end, we propose PID-Expert aggregation , an online method that adaptively combines candidate thresholds from a fixed library of PID controllers with heterogeneous parameter configurations. All experts share the same point forecasts and sequence of nonconformity scores, but each evolves according to its own history of coverage feedback. The aggregation mechanism is designed to account jointly for coverage performance and interval efficiency when adapting the relative influence of the experts. The aggregation rule raises two questions: how the expert weights compare with a fixed configuration, and how the reported interval controls miscoverage. These questions require separate arguments because the weighted average of expert miscoverage indicators generally differs from the miscoverage indicator of the aggregate threshold. We first study the comparison made by the expert weighting layer, then analyze the aggregate coverage feedback and state what the two results imply for interval efficiency.

5. Experiments 

In this section, we evaluate the proposed PID-Expert aggregation method through simulations and two real-data applications. The following experiment choices are used throughout: (a) All experiments use one-step-ahead forecasting with symmetric prediction intervals at the nominal coverage level 1 − α = 0 .9.(b) The benchmark methods are configured following Angelopoulos et al. (2023) for Con-formal PID and Gibbs and Candès (2024) for DtACI and AgACI. AgACI is imple-mented here by aggregating calibration levels; the original method aggregates interval endpoints separately (Zaffran et al., 2022). Further implementation details are pro-vided in Appendix B and Appendix C. We also include two natural variants of PID-Expert as benchmarks, using the same expert threshold trajectories: PID-Expert selection adaptively uses the threshold from whichever expert receives the highest weight in PID-Expert aggregation at each time step; PID-Expert average takes an arithmetic mean of all expert thresholds. These comparisons change how the final threshold is formed; selection reuses the aggregation weights without a separate weight update. (c) For PID-Expert aggregation, we set the multiplier step size to ν = 0 .02 and fix the width normalization scale using the initial score window, as defined in Equa-tion (3). We use the prespecified reference value V = 1 .3533 in the parameter rule 

σ = 1 /(2 L) and ξ = p(log(2 M L ) + 1) /(LV ) from Equation (10). The expert li-brary is constructed from a parameter grid around the recommended Conformal PID configuration; the grid is specified in Appendix B. (d) We assess coverage using empirical and rolling coverage and interval efficiency using mean and median width. We also report the mean scaled interval score (MSIS, Gneit-ing and Raftery, 2007), which accounts jointly for interval width and the magnitude of coverage failures. MSIS is the average interval score over the evaluation period divided by the mean absolute error of a seasonal naive forecast for seasonal series, or a one-step naive 14 forecast for nonseasonal series. Lower MSIS values indicate better interval forecasts under this scoring criterion.

6. Conclusions 

In this paper, we propose PID-Expert aggregation, an online conformal framework that adaptively combines the score thresholds generated by a heterogeneous library of PID controllers. Rather than relying on a single prespecified controller, the proposed method updates expert weights through a loss that jointly accounts for normalized interval width and expert-level miscoverage. An aggregate coverage multiplier further adjusts the relative penalty on miscoverage in response to realized coverage errors. This threshold-level aggregation provides a direct mechanism for adapting the coverage–efficiency tradeoff while preserving the distinct calibration dynamics of the individual controllers. The theoretical analysis characterizes both the aggregation and coverage properties of the method. Local regret bounds compare the weighted average of expert losses with any fixed expert over arbitrary time intervals and motivate practical reference choices for the learning rate and share parameter. Separately, the multiplier recursion yields a deterministic, pathwise upper bound on time-averaged aggregate miscoverage. Under a high-multiplier negative-drift condition, the multiplier grows sublinearly, leading to a nonasymptotic expected miscoverage bound of order O(T −1/2) above the target and almost-sure asymptotic upper control. Across four simulation settings and two real-world forecasting applications, PID-Expert consistently produces narrower mean prediction intervals than the prespecified Conformal PID benchmark while maintaining empirical coverage close to the nominal level. Its relative performance against DtACI and AgACI varies across settings, indicating that aggregating score thresholds and aggregating adaptive miscoverage levels offer complementary advan-25 tages across different data regimes. The two additional PID-Expert benchmarks clarify the coverage–efficiency tradeoff. Selection generally produces narrower intervals with lower cov-erage, whereas equal averaging produces wider intervals with higher coverage. Aggregation retains much of the width reduction from selection while generally bringing empirical cov-erage closer to the nominal level, but it does not uniformly achieve the lowest MSIS or eliminate local coverage shortfalls. Overall, the results support online aggregation as a way to reduce reliance on a single PID configuration while balancing interval width and coverage. The present study is limited to one-step-ahead symmetric intervals based on absolute-error scores and a fixed, finite controller library. Its performance therefore depends on whether the library contains controllers with suitable score scales and adaptation dy-namics. Future work could extend the framework to asymmetric tail-specific intervals, horizon-dependent aggregation for multistep forecasting, and data-driven procedures for constructing or expanding the controller library online. Such extensions would broaden the applicability of PID-Expert aggregation while retaining its central principle of jointly adapting calibration and interval efficiency. Proofs and implementation details are given in the appendices. The implementation and available data are provided at https://github. com/Guodong-Liu/PID_Expert .
