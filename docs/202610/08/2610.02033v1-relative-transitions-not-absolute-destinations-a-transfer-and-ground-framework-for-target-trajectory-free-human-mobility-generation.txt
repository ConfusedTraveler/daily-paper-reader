Abstract 

Individual mobility trajectories support urban analysis and location-based services, yet most trajectory generators require observations from their deployment city. This assumption excludes precisely the cities where trajectories are unavailable even though points of interest (POIs) and their attributes can be obtained from pub-lic maps. We study target-trajectory-free generation : learning from POIs and trajectories in source cities while utilizing only POI coor-dinates and categories in a target city, with no target trajectory or trajectory-derived statistic available for training, model selection, or generation. Existing trajectory generators typically predict ab-solute destinations, entangling reusable movement behavior with city-specific POI identities and spatial layouts. Our core insight is to replace this city-bound output with context-conditioned relative transitions. We propose Nomad , a transfer-and-ground framework that separates learning how people move from determining where those movements are realized. Specifically, a history-conditioned flow-matching model learns from source trajectories a transition prior over semantic displacement between POI contexts, geographic displacement, and elapsed time; at inference, a behavior graph and an exploration–return walk ground sampled transitions onto the target POI map. This factorization enables a direct test of represen-tation level transferability without assuming invariance of the full mobility distribution. Extensive experiments across ten cities and 14 transfers show that Nomad outperforms adaptation baselines in tra-jectory fidelity and downstream utility, lowering the average error over the best baseline of each metric by about 15% in distributional fidelity and about 3% in downstream utility. 1 Introduction 

Individual mobility trajectories support transportation and travel-demand analysis [55], epidemic simulation [7, 13], location-based services [9, 64, 65], and the study of urban function [33, 66]. How-ever, most trajectory generators assume that the deployment city already provides enough trajectories for training or adaptation. A newly deployed location-based service can only obtain the city’s map before it has accumulated local check-in histories. Alterna-tively, target trajectories may be held by another platform or insti-tution but remain inaccessible because of data privacy concerns [3]. By contrast, public mapping platforms provide POI coordinates and categories without exposing individual movements [19]. This asymmetry motivates a different question from conventional in-city generation: can a source-trained model generate target-city trajecto-ries given only the target POI map at deployment? 

We formalize these deployment conditions as a strict setting, namely target-trajectory-free . Here, target-trajectory-free does not mean that no trajectories exist in the city, but that none are available arXiv:2610.02033v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 Conference acronym ’XX, June 03–05, 2018, Woodstock, NY Trovato et al.

to the model developer. A model may leverage POIs and trajectories from one or more source cities. For the target city, it is restricted to POI-relevant information such as coordinates and categories, but it may not access any target trajectories or trajectory-derived statis-tics. The benchmark reserves target trajectories exclusively for final diagnosis and evaluation. This information boundary distinguishes the problem from domain adaptation and few-shot transfer: the target city is visible as a set of places, but its mobility behavior is unavailable. Existing methods do not naturally satisfy this boundary because they generate a city-specific object. In-city adversarial, imitation, and diffusion models learn directly from trajectories in the de-ployment city [10, 12, 14, 42, 71, 72]. Cross-city and meta-learning methods reduce the amount of target supervision, but typically re-tain target observations, a shared spatial grid, city-specific location embeddings, or a target output layer [22, 36, 56–58]. Their common prediction target is the next absolute destination 𝑝 𝑘 , whose identity and empirical frequency are defined only within one city’s POI vocabulary. When target trajectories are absent, the problem is not merely that this distribution is estimated from fewer samples: the target-specific output probabilities have never been observed. Human mobility nevertheless exhibits recurring statistics across cities, including heavy-tailed travel distances, exploration–return patterns, and repeated activity motifs [5, 17, 38, 47, 50]. These obser-vations motivate transfer, but they do not establish that complete mobility distributions are invariant across cities. Fig. 1 compares absolute destinations and relative transitions on NewYork (NYC) and Tokyo (TKY). The spatial layout and the popularity of POIs dif-fer between the two cities. In contrast, the transition distribution is highly consistent across the two cities at the level of the functional semantics of places. It demonstrates that relative transitions carry less city-specific information than absolute destinations. Motivated by this hypothesized difference, we reformulate cross-city generation around relative transitions rather than absolute destinations. We transfer a source-learned prior over context con-ditioned relative transitions to capture how people move , and then ground its samples on target-city POIs to determine where those movements are realized . Unlike an absolute destination, such a tran-sition is defined by relations between place contexts rather than by a city-private POI identity. We realize this separation with No-mad , a transfer-and-ground framework. Because POIs in different cities share neither identities nor comparable coordinates, a multi-relational graph encoder represents each POI through its category, coordinates, and spatial-semantic neighborhood, while a cross-city objective maps functionally similar contexts into a comparable rep-resentation space. A history-conditioned flow-matching model then learns from source trajectories a prior over semantic displacement, geographic displacement, and elapsed time in this space. Modeling relations rather than POI identities allows the prior to incorporate temporal and trajectory history without treating source-specific locations as prediction targets. The sampled transitions are contin-uous, whereas a target trajectory consists of discrete POIs. Nomad 

therefore snaps each sample to a target POI and aggregates these assignments into a behavior graph without accessing target trajec-tories. Walking this graph accounts for exploration, but a relative displacement cannot specify a return to a particular previously visited place. An exploration–return pointer walk handles this dis-tinction by either following a graph edge or revisiting a POI from the generated history. Thus, source trajectories determine how peo-ple move, while the target map determines where those movements can be realized. The main contributions are: 

• We formulate target-trajectory-free mobility generation and spec-ify an evaluation protocol in which the target contributes a POI map but no trajectory-derived training or generation signal. 

• We introduce a transfer-and-ground factorization that represents movement through context-conditioned semantic displacement, geographic displacement, and elapsed time, then realizes sam-pled transitions on the target POI map through behavior-graph grounding and exploration–return modeling. 

• Across 10 cities and 14 source-to-target transfers, Nomad im-proves trajectory fidelity by about 15% and downstream train-on-synthetic–test-on-real utility by about 3% over six baselines. Controlled analyses further show that relative transitions contain less city-specific information under matched contexts and incur less cross-city degradation than absolute-destination generation, directly supporting the proposed mechanism.

2.2 Transferability of Transitions 

Definition 2.3 forbids any target trajectory, so 𝑓 can rely only on structure that is shared across cities. We argue that this shared structure lies in the transitions between places rather than in the absolute places visited. and we accordingly read a trajectory as a chain of transitions.

We describe it by three quantities that do not name an absolute destination, namely a representation of the change of place context, the ground displacement between the two POIs, and the elapsed time Δ𝑡 =

𝑡 𝑘 − 𝑡 𝑘 −1. A trajectory is then a chain of such transitions. The task above transfer what is learned from source trajectories to a city that has none. 3 Methodology 

In this section, we present a three-stage framework Nomad for target-trajectory-free generation, as illustrated in Fig. 2. 

3.1 Cross-City POI Alignment 

The goal of this stage is to approximate Assumption 2.5, which embeds the POIs of every city into one shared semantic space. What defines the role of a POI is not its identity but its attributes and the places around it. We therefore build the POIs of each city into a multi-relational graph, which edges are induced by spatial location, and node features are derived from the category semantics and geographic coordinates. Finally, we encode this graph with a Relation-aware Graph Attention Network to capture the shared representation underlying functionally similar POIs across cities. 

Multi-relational POIs Graph. Specifically, We connect the POIs of each city with three relations. We first partition the city into hexagonal cells, following the standard way in spatial analysis [60]. Each cell covers a compact local area of roughly one square kilometer. Within a cell, we link each POI to its nearest neighbors, so that places close to each other are connected. Across neighboring cells, we link each POI to its nearest neighbors, so that its wider spatial surroundings are connected. For each POI, we also link the nearest POIs of the same category, so that places of similar role are connected. All three kinds of edges are built by nearest-neighbor search over geographic distance. 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Settings 

4.1.1 Baselines. We compare our method with six adaptation base-lines. Markov [16] is a category-level Markov chain that is es-timated on the source and grounded to the nearest target POIs. 

DITRAS [37] is a diary-based generator with category-level tran-sitions and a gravity-style grounding that places each step by tar-get POI density and distance. Gravity [1] is the classic spatial-interaction model whose distance-decay exponents are fit on the source and applied to the target. MIRAGE [12] is a graph-enhanced discrete diffusion model that we adapt to the target city by map-ping the output representations to the nearest POIs in the target city. COLA [58] is a cross-city Mobility Transformer whose output vocabulary is extended to the target POIs, and GTG [56] is a graph Transformer with domain adaptation capability. Here the statistics from source cities are as its input to adapt our zero-shot setting. 

4.1.2 Evaluation Metrics. Following standard practice [11, 12], we evaluate the generation results in two aspects. One aspect is the 

downstream task utility based on “training on synthetic data, testing on real data”. We train prediction and recommendation models using the generated trajectories, test them on a real target set, and report the gap between these models and those trained on real data. The prediction models include FPMC [46], BERT4Rec [51], SRGNN [61], and SASRec [23]. Their gaps are averaged into Pred-MAPE 

and Pred-MSPE . The recommendation models include DMF [62], MultiVAE [30], NeuMF [20], and BPR [45]. Their gaps are averaged into Rec-MAPE and Rec-MSPE . The other aspect is distributional similarity between generated and real trajectories. OD-Sim is the similarity of the origin-destination flow. It reflects where trips start and end. Three metrics are Jensen-Shannon divergences between the generated and the real distributions. Distance compares the lengths of moves. Speed compares how fast people move. Time-Cat compares which category of place is visited at which hour. 

4.2 Main Results 

The comparisons of different methods on the four transfer settings are shown in Table 1. Nomad achieves the best results on both downstream utility and distributional fidelity in all four settings. Across all 14 transfer settings, ten of which are reported in Appen-dix E.1, Nomad lowers the average error over the best baseline of each metric by about 15% on distributional fidelity and about 3% on downstream utility. Specifically, the average gain over the eight metrics reaches more than 33% on the {KYO+NGO →OSA} trans-fer. Among the baselines, the neural methods including MIRAGE ,

GTG and COLA tend to perform better on the utility metrics, while the classical models Markov , DITRAS and Gravity are stronger on the distributional metrics. The former can model complex spa-tiotemporal distributions and learn sequence-level semantics, which benefits downstream tasks. The latter are built directly on trajectory statistics, so they capture the overall trends more easily. Nomad 

contains both ingredients, pairing a neural generator that models transitions at the sequence level with a behavior graph that aggre-gates their statistics over the target POIs. In addition, comparing the two Japanese transfers in Table 1 and the Turkish settings in Appendix E.1, moving from a single source city to more source cities changes the results only marginally. More source trajectories bring limited gains to target-trajectory-free generation. This sug-gests that the knowledge Nomad models is shared across cities, and a small set of source cities is enough to learn it. 

4.3 Ablation Study 

4.3.1 Ablation of Different Modules. To evaluate the efficacy of 

Nomad ’s components, we conduct an ablation study comparing five variants (details in Appendix C.2). Table 2 reports results on four transfers, demonstrating that deleting any module leads to performance declines, underscoring their critical contributions. Re-moving flow-matching hurts distributional metrics most, because the basic regressor keeps only mean movement and inflates Dis-tance divergence. Conversely, removing the copy-gate barely moves distributions but raises Recommendation error, since walks without revisits lose the repeat structure recommenders learn. Removing the auto-regressive mask breaks step order and blows up predic-tion error. The graph encoder and history-condition underpin both aspects. Dropping either raises error across the board. 

4.3.2 Ablation of Data Efficiency. We test how the error changes as an increasing fraction of the target trajectories is used for adap-tation. We concatenate the source-city trajectories with a growing fraction of the target-city trajectories and train Nomad on the mixture. This moves the model from the target-trajectory-free set-ting to cross-city generation as more target trajectories are added. Figure 3 reports the eight metrics on four transfers. First, the dis-tributional errors decrease steadily as more target trajectories are used, because the statistics of the real trajectories can be collected and learned directly. Downstream utility errors fluctuate, yet the overall trend remains downward. Second, across these transfer set-tings, only a small fraction of the target data brings the error close to the in-domain lower bound. This shows the transferred move-ment pattern covers most knowledge these tasks need. Third, the Metropolis setting behaves differently. Its POI vocabulary is larger, so its utility error keeps decreasing as more target data is added. This shows in-domain trajectories still help at larger data scales. 5 Conclusion 

This paper investigates the problem of target-trajectory-free gener-ation for those cities without accessible trajectories. The primary challenge lies in transferring movement knowledge across cities that share no POIs. To tackle this challenge, we propose Nomad, a transfer-and-ground framework. Our approach models relative tran-sitions rather than absolute destinations with a flow-matching prior, and grounds them onto the target POIs with a behavior-graph and a pointer network. Extensive experiments across fourteen transfers confirm that Nomad outperforms adaptation baselines in distribu-tional fidelity and downstream utility. Controlled analyses further show that relative transitions contain less city-specific information under matched contexts.

B.2 Cross-City Transfer for Mobility 

Cross-city transfer methods reuse mobility knowledge from data-rich cities. Most effort targets spatiotemporal prediction. Meta learn-ing and region re-weighting adapt a source model to a target city [15, 22, 36, 57]. Cross-city representation learning aligns regions or knowledge-graph embeddings for downstream reuse [8, 69]. These methods act over a shared spatial grid rather than a discrete POI vo-cabulary, and still fit part of the model on target-city observations. Cross-city trajectory generation has been attempted recently, but the closest methods such as COLA, GTG, and CRAFT [29, 56, 58] remain bound to a target-specific location space and still require trajectories from the target city. Generating individual check-in trajectories for a city without trajectories remains challenging. 

C Implementation Details C.1 Datasets Details 

We evaluate cross-city zero-shot generation on public Foursquare check-in data [63, 64] covering ten cities in three regions. New York City (NYC) and Tokyo (TKY) are large metropolises. Taken together, the full pairwise results show that Nomad transfers reliably across metropolitan, Japanese, and Turkish city groups, with its most consistent advantage lying in preserving the physical and temporal characteristics of mobility while remaining competitive for downstream use. 

E.2 Parameter Analysis 

The supplemented sensitivity analysis further provides the impacts of three coefficients in Nomad regulate complementary compo-nents of the framework. Specifically, 𝜆 cat weights the category pre-diction loss for cross-city semantic alignment, 𝜆 link controls the link reconstruction loss that preserves the structural relationships among POIs, and 𝜆 gate balances the return gate and revisit pre-diction objectives in the Pointer Network. Across both transfer settings, competitive performance is maintained over a broad range of parameter combinations rather than being confined to a single sharp optimum, indicating that Nomad is not highly sensitive to fine-grained tuning. Noticeable degradation mainly occurs when either 𝜆 cat or 𝜆 link becomes overly dominant, as excessive emphasis on category semantics or graph structure can weaken the balance re-quired for learning transferable POI representations. In comparison, varying 𝜆 gate causes only moderate changes in the error landscape, suggesting that the return-and-exploration mechanism remains sta-ble under different relative weights.
