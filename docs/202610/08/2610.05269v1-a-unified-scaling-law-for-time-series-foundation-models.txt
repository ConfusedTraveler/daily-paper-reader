# ABSTRACT 

We develop a Unified Scaling Law and a Unified Theory of Time Series Learning to understand how model capacity and historical information support forecasting. Across different lookback lengths and forecast horizons, we analyze 18,768 ex-perimental cells from 21 checkpoints on 23 dataset-frequency tasks spanning six domains. Our empirical methodology integrates local resource relations into a par-simonious, fitted five-parameter law: capacity gains increase with history, context gains diminish toward saturation, and horizon effects enter as a common shift. Fit-ted without Toto 2.0, the law predicts its horizon-averaged capacity-scaling curves with mean absolute percentage errors of 1.09% and 1.50% at input lengths 2048 and 4096. To understand how history supports prediction, our learning theory uses Gaussian regression to analyze rule identification and predictive capability. We hypothesize that full-shot models learn by accumulating information in weights ,while frozen time series foundation models (TSFMs) use history by extracting information through activations . Matched-history comparisons establish the pre-dictive value of additional history. Controlled parameter exchanges and activation interventions provide evidence that history-derived rule information can be re-tained, reused across queries, and used to recover a contribution to long-context prediction. Together, these findings inform capacity scaling, context allocation, and the development of models that retain and apply historical rules. Code and main results are available at https://github.com/Fifthky/UniScale .

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Time series foundation models (TSFMs), large neural models pretrained from scratch on time series, are becoming more capable as forecasting research expands (Ansari et al., 2024; Das et al., 2024; Woo et al., 2024; Khwaja et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). Public benchmarks such as GIFT-Eval and fev-bench bring together a growing range of forecasting models (Aksu et al., 2024; Shchur et al., 2026), while application studies investigate their use in financial forecasting, precipitation predic-tion, and battery degradation (Rahimikia et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). This progress has intensified debate over the value and scalability of foundation forecasting. Recent cri-tiques question the premise of domain universality (Dai et al., 2026a). Empirical studies show that selected supervised baselines can compete with TSFMs under particular tasks and history budgets (Xu et al., 2025; Zhang & Gilpin, 2026). Alongside these critiques, early scaling studies establish benefits from increasing training resources and identify interactions between model complexity and history (Shi et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025; Edwards et al., 2024). This raises the question: under what resource and forecasting conditions does pretrained scale translate into useful predictive gains? Existing work establishes training-resource scaling (Yao et al., 2025; Edwards et al., 2024), characterizes interactions of capacity and lookback (Shi et al., 2024), and studies how longer con-texts support forecasting (Liu et al., 2025b; Butera et al., 2026). However, these works have not fully resolved the ongoing debate because their experiments remain limited in either scale (model and dataset diversity) or scope (pretrained capacity, available history, and forecasting horizon). This 1arXiv:2610.05269v1 [cs.LG] 4 Oct 2026 Preprint debate calls for more general and scalable quantification across heterogeneous TSFMs to analyze error under a wide range of tasks. Therefore, we propose a Unified Scaling Law , aiming to deepen understanding of how TSFMs turn pretrained capacity and history information into useful forecasts, and to inform their future training and application. We fitted the Law through controlled experiments. The protocol yields 18,768 dataset–configuration–checkpoint cells and 1,070 aggregated response points from 21 checkpoints on 23 dataset-frequency tasks across six domains. Public results from GIFT-Eval provide cross-source evaluation on the same task panel. Our empirical methodology uses local resource relations and Occam’s razor to guide law construction, parameter estimation, and predictive validation. As a case study, we withhold the Toto 2.0 series of five same-architecture checkpoints, spanning approxi-mately 4M to 2.5B parameters, and use the law estimated from the remaining heterogeneous check-points to predict its capacity-scaling response. The Mean Absolute Percentage Errors (MAPEs) of the horizon-averaged capacity curves are 1.09% and 1.50% with input lengths of 2048 and 4096, showing the Law remains informative when applied to a held-out architecture. Further, to understand why and how TSFMs work and how they relate to full-shot models, we pro-pose a Unified Theory of Time Series Learning . History carries information about both the current state and the rule governing its evolution. We hypothesize that models trained on the target series (full-shot models) learn by accumulating information in weights , while frozen TSFMs use history by extracting information through activations , inspired by earlier work (Russin et al., 2025; Zhang & Gilpin, 2025; Xu et al., 2025). We analyze this learning problem through Gaussian regression and investigate the hypotheses through experiments matching the full-shot training-record length to the TSFM input length, complemented by controlled tests of rule identification, parameter reten-tion, and cross-query activation transfer. The empirical methodology and learning theory provide a reusable basis for future scaling research as models and forecasting conditions evolve. Our main contributions are: • A unified scaling law and empirical research methodology. We develop a Unified Scaling Law linking capacity, input length, and output horizon through extensive experiments. We contribute an empirical methodology from local resource analysis to law construction, estimation, and vali-dation on external public results and a held-out case study. • A unified theory of time series learning. We combine Gaussian regression analysis with hy-potheses about how TSFMs and full-shot models turn historical evidence into predictive rules. Matched-history comparisons and controlled parameter and activation interventions examine how rule information is retained and applied to new queries. • Guidance for model builders and users. We use the Unified Scaling Law and learning analysis to give model developers empirical support for pretraining larger models. We guide users in selecting models, input lengths, and adaptation strategies for their forecasting requirements and deployment constraints. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Towards Time Series Foundation Models. Forecasting has progressed from statistical methods to Transformer and linear models (Nie et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2026c), and to TSFMs pretrained across datasets (Ansari et al., 2024; Das et al., 2024; Woo et al., 2024; Khwaja et al., 2026). Benchmarks compare these models (Aksu et al., 2024; Shchur et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026), alongside debate over domain universality (Dai et al., 2026a). Comparisons with supervised models and history-based predictors highlight the roles of available history and adapta-tion (Xu et al., 2025; Zhang & Gilpin, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang & Gilpin, 2026; Dai et al., 2026b). Studies of in-weight learning and in-context inference offer a perspective on information use (Russin et al., 2025; Faw et al., 2025). We connect learning hypotheses with matched-history experi-ments and the Unified Scaling Law to study how TSFMs use forecasting resources. Our cross-query interventions examine whether historical rule information remains useful on independent queries.

# 6 A U NIFIED THEORY OF TIME SERIES LEARNING 

Our Unified Theory of Time Series Learning studies how full-shot models and frozen TSFMs turn historical evidence into predictive rules. Historical evidence supports rule identification, the predic-tor’s capabilities determine how accurately it estimates and applies the rule, and the learning path determines whether rule information is retained after training or extracted within the current fore-cast. Building on in-weight and in-context learning (Russin et al., 2025; Sander et al., 2024), we combine a regression analysis with two learning hypotheses and experiments on rule identification, retention, and reuse across queries. 6.1 HISTORICAL EVIDENCE AND THE TWO LEARNING PATHS

Full-shot models train on pre-origin history with local input ℓ0 = 96 and retain fitted states during scoring; TSFMs receive length-L histories at the same origins. This varies the shared history budget in earlier comparisons (Zhang & Gilpin, 2025; Xu et al., 2025); Appendix E.1 details implementation and training. Both full-shot architectures improve task-averaged MASE at every tested history extension and horizon (Figure 4). At H = 96 , increasing history from 1,024 to 8,192 reduces DLinear’s MASE from 1.042 to 0.931 and PatchTST’s from 0.885 to 0.833. The fixed TSFM panel’s mean and median remain lower at every matched condition; PatchTST’s gaps to both narrow over this history range at all three horizons. A complementary crossed experiment varies training history T ∈ { 4096 , 8192 } and immediate in-put C ∈ { 96 , 512 } independently at H = 96 , on the same 23 tasks with three seeds. Alongside DLinear and two PatchTST sizes, we compare the same TimesFM 2.5 checkpoint before and af-ter task-specific adaptation, with each input configuration trained and validation-selected separately. MASE falls from 0.9233 to 0.7877 under adaptation, a 14.68% reduction in the task-averaged score. Appendix E.1 reports the results. The MASE gap between the 96-step and 512-step TimesFM configurations decreases from 0.2396 before adaptation to 0.0768 afterwards, a reduction of about 68%. We intervene inside frozen TimesFM 2.5 using activation patching (Zhang & Nanda, 2024). We average four independent donors’ intermediate-state differences between 512 observations and their final 96 observations. The primary layer, token span, history length, and strength are fixed across processes and experiments. For transfer , we add this historical state difference to the short-input state of a new query. For recovery , we replace the corresponding state in the query’s natural long-context execution with its short-input state, then add the same donor difference; other long-context states remain unchanged. Transfer tests cross-query reuse; recovery tests restoration of its contribu-tion to natural long-context prediction. Transfer reduces mean error by 7.51%; independent-donor recovery restores 41.67% of the mean error increase caused by erasure at the selected site. Erasure raises error, and transfer and recovery improve it with positive paired-query 95% intervals in all 18 conditions. Both interventions out-perform random and carrier-only controls; rule-dependent gains also hold against shuffled-history donors (Appendix E.5). With recipient query and history fixed, matching-rule donors outperform opposite-rule donors for both interventions in every tested TimesFM condition across three layers and three strengths. Across the nine layer–strength settings, mean recovery improves over erasure in all nine and mean direct transfer over query-only prediction in seven. Chronos 2 shows the same matching advantage on independent confirmation data at a development-selected setting: recovery lowers mean error by 6.01%, with positive intervals in all 18 conditions, while direct transfer leaves overall error nearly unchanged. # 8 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Discussion. The Unified Scaling Law describes average resource returns, while our learning anal-ysis distinguishes evidence available in history from its predictive use. Retention and extraction provide complementary ways to apply historical rules. Task-specific adaptation can shift the use-ful balance between retained information and immediate input. These findings motivate evaluating history-use capability alongside capacity and jointly validating training and deployment choices. 

Conclusion. We establish a five-parameter Unified Scaling Law through observed resource prop-erties, parsimony, and validation on Public results and held-out Toto 2.0 checkpoints. Our Unified Theory of Time Series Learning combines Gaussian regression with matched-history comparisons and controlled interventions. Parameter exchanges support retained rule information, while cross-query activation transfer and recovery support its reuse in frozen prediction. The empirical method-ology provides a reusable sequence from local resource analysis to estimation and validation. # A DETAILED RELATED WORKS 

A.1 TIME SERIES FOUNDATION MODELS 

Time series modeling supports diverse applications, including financial forecasting, precipitation prediction, and battery monitoring (Rahimikia et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2025). Time series forecasting has expanded from statistical methods to Transformer-based models such as PatchTST and iTransformer (Nie et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024) and linear models such as DLinear, FITS, and TimePrism (Zeng et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2026c), alongside the recent rise of large-scale pretraining (Kottapalli et al., 2025). Chronos casts probabilistic prediction as token modeling (Ansari et al., 2024), TimesFM develops a decoder-only forecaster (Das et al., 2024), and Moirai introduces unified training across datasets and frequencies (Woo et al., 2024). Subsequent releases include Chronos-Bolt and Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2024; 2025), Moirai-MoE and Moirai 2 (Liu et al., 2025a; 2026a), and the TiRex and TiRex-2 forecasting models (Auer et al., 2025; Podest et al., 2026). Other models include Sundial (Liu et al., 2025c), Granite FlowState (Graf et al., 2026), and Granite PatchTST-FM (Wen et al., 2026), alongside the Toto and Toto 2.0 series (Cohen et al., 2025; Khwaja et al., 2026). Time-MoE and Timer-S1 explore expert-based scaling (Shi et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026c), while Timer-XL develops expanded forecasting contexts (Liu et al., 2025b); other released forecasters include Falcon-X, Kairos, YingLong, and Reverso (Liu et al., 2026b; Feng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). The public comparison also covers TabPFN-TS (Hoo et al., 2026), and the forecasting releases t0, CleanTS, goia, PatchFM, and LiteSpecFormer (Meyer et al., 2026a; EINK, n.d.; Gredio Labs, n.d.; vilhess, n.d.; FlowVortex, 2026). GIFT-Eval and fev-bench provide comparisons across heterogeneous domains, frequencies, and forecast lengths (Aksu et al., 2024; Shchur et al., 2026). A.2 SCALING LAWS FOR LANGUAGE AND TIME SERIES MODELS 

Language-model scaling laws characterize loss through parameters, training data, and computation (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). Test-time scaling extends the allocation problem to inference (Snell et al., 2025). For time series, Shi et al. (2024) jointly analyze data size, model complexity, and lookback with a fixed forecast horizon, finding that useful lookback depends on the training-data regime. Yao et al. (2025) study parameter, data, and compute scaling across in-domain and out-of-domain forecasting, while Edwards et al. (2024) study training-resource laws with a fixed context. Complementary studies examine predictability and context selection. Butera et al. (2026) explain why identifying a generating process can require history longer than its memory, while Sander et al. (2024) analyze how Transformers estimate autoregressive dynamics from con-text. Wang et al. (2026) relate the lowest forecasting errors attained by deep models to window-wise pattern complexity, while Huang et al. (2026) show that the relationship between useful lookback and forecast horizon varies across datasets for tuned linear models. Timer-XL examines longer contexts and the dependence of their benefits on model and training choices (Liu et al., 2025b). Toto 2.0 demonstrates improved forecasting performance with increas-ing model size and examines how capacity affects long-horizon stability (Khwaja et al., 2026). YingLong studies output scaling (Wang et al., 2025). Diversified inference investigates sampling and perturbations alongside capacity and context length (Hua et al., 2026). These findings con-cern different architectures and inference procedures, without establishing a shared error law across heterogeneous released checkpoints. A.3 ANALYSIS AND CONTROVERSY SURROUNDING TSFM S

The growth of foundation forecasting has prompted scrutiny of its conceptual scope and evaluation (Dai et al., 2026a; Meyer et al., 2026b). Empirical comparisons examine the performance of pre-trained forecasting models across model scales and application domains (Chakraborty et al., 2026; Alonso & Franklin, 2026). Xu et al. (2025) and Zhang & Gilpin (2025) compare frozen TSFMs with supervised baselines given the same history, using autoregressive models on forecasting bench-marks and N-BEATS on chaotic systems, respectively. Zhang et al. (2025) combine a representation learned by principal component analysis on a multi-domain corpus with a linear head trained on the available history, questioning the practical necessity of zero-shot prediction. Context parroting 16 Preprint shows that matching and copying historical segments can rival TSFMs on dynamical-system fore-casting and relates one-step error to history length (Zhang & Gilpin, 2026). QuitoBench and FETS report context- and horizon-dependent model comparisons on application-traffic and energy data, respectively (Xue et al., 2026; Obermeier et al., 2026). These studies establish the importance of history budgets and task-specific adaptation. An information-use perspective connects these comparisons to learning mechanisms. Russin et al. (2025) distinguish incremental in-weight learning from in-context inference through activation dy-namics. In forecasting, Faw et al. (2025) investigate adaptation through examples supplied in con-text and through weight fine-tuning, while Butera et al. (2026) explain how training or inference supports identification of a generating process. Rank and compressibility analyses examine how TSFMs represent predictive information (Yu et al., 2026). We develop learning hypotheses about history information and examine them through matched-history experiments with linear and Trans-former full-shot models (Zeng et al., 2023; Nie et al., 2023).
