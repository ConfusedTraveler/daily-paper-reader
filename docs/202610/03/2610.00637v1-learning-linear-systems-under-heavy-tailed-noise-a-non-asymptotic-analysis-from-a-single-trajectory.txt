Abstract 

We establish non-asymptotic sample complexity bounds for the least-squares estimation of vector autoregressive models for exponentially stable systems with heavy-tailed noise based on a single observed trajectory. By assuming i.i.d. noise, bounded noise covariance, and persistent excitation, we show that the estimation error is eO(r1/2T −1/2+1 /p ) under bounded pth moment for p > 2, where T is the number of samples, r is the noise dimension, and eO(·) hides logarithmic terms. We also introduce a unifying approach to sample complexity analysis applicable to broad classes of noise distributions and showcase this by deriving error bounds for sub-exponential and sub-Gaussian noise distributions. Finally, we specialize our analysis to autoregressive models with exogenous inputs and show that the dimension factor of the error bound is independent of the model order. 

# 1 Introduction 

System identification has received renewed attention in recent years due to its central role in data-driven model-based control and decision-making algorithms (Dean et al., 2017; Simchowitz et al., 2018; Oymak and Ozay, 2019; Tsiamis et al., 2022; Ziemann et al., 2024; Faradonbeh et al., 2017; Bakshi et al., 2023; Kanakeri and Mitra, 2024; Sattar et al., 2025). In many applications—ranging from robotics (Rosolia et al., 2018) to process control (Qin and Badgwell, 1997)— one must learn a dynamical model from data in order to design controllers that are both sample-efficient and constraint-aware. In particular, model predictive control (MPC) relies explicitly on system models often learned via system identification to make predictions and optimize control actions (Rawlings et al., 2017; Qin and Badgwell, 1997). This paradigm contrasts with direct policy learning approaches

1arXiv:2610.00637v1 [cs.LG] 30 Sep 2026 (Fazel et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2023; Sutton and Barto, 2018), where control policies are learned end-to-end without explicitly learning the underlying system dynamics. When data are limited and safety or operational constraints must be respected, model-based control—learning the system model with system identification and subsequently synthesizing a controller (e.g., robust control or MPC)—is often the only viable approach. Classical system identification theory has primarily focused on asymptotic properties of estimators, establishing consistency and convergence as the number of samples tends to infinity (Ljung, 1998). While these results establish basic properties of the estimator, they are insufficient for understanding performance in the finite-sample regime relevant to learning-based control. In order to analyze end-to-end performance and to compare different paradigms, non-asymptotic guarantees of estimation error for a finite number of samples are crucial, and a growing body of recent work has developed non-asymptotic sample complexity bounds for linear system identification (Dean et al., 2017; Simchowitz et al., 2018; Oymak and Ozay, 2019; Ziemann et al., 2024). Most existing non-asymptotic results on ordinary least-squares (OLS) rely on strong assumptions regarding the noise distribution . In particular, the majority of current analyses assume that the system noise is sub-Gaussian (Dean et al., 2017; Simchowitz et al., 2018; Oymak and Ozay, 2019; Ziemann et al., 2024) while few focus on the sub-Weibull class(Faradonbeh et al., 2017), which excludes distributions with polynomial tail bounds such as Pareto or Student’s t-distributions. Heavy-tailed disturbances arise naturally in applications involving outliers, intermittent shocks, or imperfect sensing and actuation, such as power grid blackout frequency that is often modeled by the Pareto distribution (Nair et al., 2022). The existing theoretical guarantees based on light-tailed noise distributions and exponentially decaying heavy-tailed distributions are inapplicable in such scenarios. This motivates the central question of this paper: Does model-based control remain statistically viable when system noise exhibits heavy tails? 

We address this question by studying the non-asymptotic sample complexity of OLS estimators for linear dynamical systems under heavy-tailed noise distributions. Specifically, we focus on (i) OLS estimator, (ii) identification from single trajectory, (iii) exponentially stable system, and (iv) general heavy-tailed noise distributions for bounded pth moment with p > 2. Rationale for these settings is as follows: OLS is often employed in practice as the go-to method even when the noise covariance is unknown due to its computational tractability and is the basis for a variety of practical algorithms (Qin and Badgwell, 1997). Single trajectory is often the most realistic data collection scenario in practice, as one typically has access to only one realization of the system evolution. Most controllers designed by engineers are exponentially stable for reliability and performance guarantees, such as power system frequency and voltage control, process control, and robotics. Finally, relaxing light-tailed noise assumptions is crucial for understanding the robustness of model-based approaches in real-world settings where the system is subject to heavy-tailed noises. 

Contributions This paper provides a non-asymptotic sample complexity analysis of OLS-based linear system identification on the vector autoregressive (VAR) model for exponentially stable system under heavy-tailed noise distributions with bounded pth moment with p > 2. We present a unifying approach for non-asymptotic sample complexity analysis that accommodates a broad class of noise distributions and yields explicit finite-sample error bounds expressed in terms of general system properties. Finally, we derive non-asymptotic estimation error upper bounds for heavy-tailed distributions with bounded pth moment for p > 2 and demonstrate its applicability to sub-exponential and sub-Gaussian distributions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to provide comprehensive non-asymptotic sample complexity guarantees for (i) OLS estimators, (ii) 2from single trajectory, (iii) for exponentially stable systems, (iv) under general heavy-tailed noise distributions. By assuming exponential stability, persistent excitation, independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) noise, and bounded propagation of disturbances, we show that the estimation error is bounded by eO(r1/2T −1/2+1 /p ) under bounded pth moment with p > 2, where T is the sample size and r is the noise dimension. Notably, our analysis decouples the noise and state dimensions to address certain classes of rank-deficient systems, where our bound is expressed entirely in r and can be smaller than the state dimension. Our analysis provides an explicit characterization not only of the error scale but also of the dependence of system-specific constants that characterize stability, persistent excitation, and covariance bound. This clearly reveals the effect of these properties on the parameter estimation error. Our analysis demonstrates that for p > 2 distributions, the estimation error can be controlled by increasing the number of samples, albeit at a slower rate than in the sub-Gaussian case. This suggests that model-based approaches remain statistically viable even in the presence of moderately heavy-tailed (bounded pth moment with p > 2) noise distributions .Our technique uses a fast mixing argument (via exponential stability) and a blocking strategy by introducing stacking of noise vectors, which improves upon previous works (Yu, 1994) by removing temporal correlation completely. The estimation error can then be expressed in terms of the empirical covariance tail bound of the augmented noise distribution, where standard concentration inequalities for independent random variables can be applied. Another contribution, also at a more technical level, is deriving a tighter sample complexity bound for autoregressive with exogenous input (ARX) model identification. We specialize our general analysis to ARX models and show that, under suitable excitation and stability conditions, the dimension factor of the sample complexity depends only on the combined state and input dimension and is independent of the autoregressive order. This result is an improvement over existing bounds that scale with the order of the ARX model (Ziemann et al., 2024), where the complexity scales with q1/2 and q is the order of the ARX model. 

Related Work Previous works dedicated to non-asymptotic sample complexity analysis have primarily focused on sub-Gaussian noise and generalized results for exponentially stable systems to marginally stable systems. Dean et al. (2017) first explored convergence of finite samples with multi-trajectory inputs. This was extended to single trajectory by Oymak and Ozay (2019) for the Markov parameter of the state-space model. Tsiamis et al. (2022) relaxed the noise distribution to i.i.d. sub-Gaussian noise, and is generalized by Ziemann et al. (2024); Simchowitz et al. (2018) to non-explosive or marginally stable systems. In particular, Simchowitz et al. (2018) avoids the mixing time argument and shows that marginally stable systems can be easier to estimate due to better signal-to-noise ratio. In comparison, our work focuses on exponentially stable systems and heavy-tailed noise and does not address marginally stable systems. Theoretical guarantees for sample complexity of systems affected by heavy-tailed distributions are limited. Perhaps most similar to our work is Faradonbeh et al. (2017), which explored OLS with sub-Weibull noise on a stable system, and obtained eO(T −1/2d1/2). However, their analysis only addressed distributions with exponentially decaying tails and not polynomially decaying tails. Our work extends the scope of noise distributions, such as Pareto and Student’s t-distributions, which can be captured by bounded pth moment assumptions. Sattar et al. (2025) also conducted analysis for OLS and relaxed the noise distribution to only bounded covariance but made a restrictive bounded input assumption. Recent work by Bakshi et al. (2023) considered noise distributions with only bounded 4th moment and guaranteed an upper bound of eO(T −1/2). The estimator studied therein requires the solution of a constraint system that is only guaranteed to be feasible under non-trivial 3conditions, thus limiting its applicability. Kanakeri and Mitra (2024) established a similar bound with a robust linear least-squares algorithm, but only for multi-trajectory data. We summarize existing results and compare to our work below. We first find an explicit form of ϵ using Lemma 4 by deriving the empirical covariance tail bound of the augmented noise vectors in (12) using the Markov inequality and bounded pth moment property in Proposition 2. Our sample complexity bound retains the dimension scale r1/2 similar to the sub-Gaussian distributions in Theorem 3 and sub-exponential distributions in Theorem 4. Additionally, the bound is expressed in terms of the noise dimension r,not the system dimension d, whereas previous works have established the bound with respect to 

d. Thus, our result can be more advantageous for certain rank-deficient systems where r < d . See Section 5 on the ARX model for more discussion. Different from the sample complexity bounds recovered for light-tailed distributions, the heavy-tailed result deteriorates by a factor of T 1/p , and the error depends on 1/δ rather than log(1 /δ ). More precisely, the error depends on (1 /δ )1/p log(1 /δ ), which converges to log(1 /δ ) when all moments are finite. The estimation error also increases polynomially with κ, the upper bound of the pth moment. When p → ∞ , the error bound matches with that of light-tailed distributions, which is expected as they have infinitely many bounded moments. As p → 2, the estimation deteriorates by T 1/p ,indicating slower convergence when the number of bounded moments is limited and the tail of the noise distribution becomes heavier.

Remark 3. Compared to the existing error bounds for ARX model identification in the literature, our result in Theorem 2 has the following advantage: Theorem 2 does not explicitly depend on the order of the ARX model q. Thus, when the model order is large, our result enjoys a tighter sample complexity bound than the existing results that depend on q explicitly (Ziemann et al., 2024). This improvement was made by decoupling the system dimension d and the noise dimension r in (1), and by expressing the sample complexity in terms of r instead of d. For the ARX model in (16), the noise dimension r = dy + du is substantially smaller than the system dimension d = q(dy + du) when the model order q is large. Note that system-dependent coefficients can be dependent of q. Specifically, the q dependence appears in the stability coefficient L due to explicit bounding of A, which can be removed if expressed in terms of the system matrices. When T becomes large, specifically T ≥ α−tc ,the dependence on tc becomes negligible, as expressed in (8b). 

# 6 Conclusion 

This work analyzed non-asymptotic sample complexity of linear system identification concerning exponentially stable systems subjected to heavy-tailed noise distributions with bounded pth moment for p > 2. We provide a unifying lemma applicable to general noise distributions, and we further demonstrate our approach to sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential distributions, establishing compet-itive sample complexity bounds for each noise distribution class. Our results reveal that sample complexity depends on the controllability properties of the system and the tail behavior of the noise distribution. Specifically, we found that for systems affected by heavy-tailed distributions, the sample complexity deteriorates with decreasing number of bounded moments p. Lastly, we specialized our results to the ARX model, where the model dimension of the parameter error does not explicitly depend on the autoregressive order, thus demonstrating that our framework can improve upon existing results in literature. 11 There remain open questions from our work. Our technique relies on a mixing time argument due to exponential stability assumption, which can be avoided for OLS of light-tailed noise (Simchowitz et al., 2018; Ziemann et al., 2024). Can this also be avoided for heavy-tailed noise? Additionally, the 

T 1/p dependence of heavy-tailed distribution suggested by our theoretical results is not corroborated by the numerical results. This suggests that our approach, applying a truncation technique on a Markov inequality, may be loose, and it is possible that a more refined analysis can yield a tighter bound. Future work includes deriving the lower sample complexity bound, extending our analysis to marginally stable systems, and addressing heavy-tailed noise distributions with bounded pth moment for p ≤ 2.

# G Numerical Results 

Simulation Design We performed numerical simulations of the ARX model to verify the sample complexity bounds we obtained theoretically for distributions with bounded pth moments in Theo-rem 1, sub-exponential distributions in Theorem 4, and sub-Gaussian distributions in Theorem 3. We explore an ARX model defined in (16) with autoregressive order q = 5 and dimensions dy = du = 10 .For all simulations, the input signal ut = vt follows a Gaussian distribution with unit standard deviation, and the following noise distributions for wt are considered: • sub-Gaussian distribution: Gaussian distribution, • sub-exponential distribution: Laplace distribution, • Bounded pth moment with p > 2: Student’s t distribution ( p = 2 .1, 4.1), Pareto distribution (p = 2 .1, 4.1), • No Bounded Moments: Cauchy distribution. We set all noise distributions with unit standard deviation except for distributions without finite covari-ance. For each noise distribution and sample sizes T = [100 , 200 , 500 , 10 3, 10 4, 4×10 4, 10 5, 10 6, 5×10 6],we ran 200 simulations generating random single-trajectory data. Discussion Our results are shown in Figure 2. Below 1000 samples, T is not sufficiently large to exceed the thresholds in our theoretical results, so the relative errors are much larger. To verify the theoretical sample complexity bounds when the failure probability δ is small, we plot error bounds for the 99 th, 95 th, 90 th percentiles of the relative error, where the deviation from the T −1/2 rate is not significant. For Gaussian and Laplace distributions, the sample complexity exhibits a clear 

T −1/2 dependence compared with the reference line. As the number of moments decrease, Pareto and Student’s t distributions with p = 2 .1 at the 99 th percentile exhibit larger variance, where the fitted slope is −0.43 for Student’s t and −0.46 for Pareto distributions. While Theorem 1 predicts that the sample complexity upper bound should deteriorate by T 1/p , our numerical results do not clearly indicate whether these distributions actually have slower convergence. Finally, we explore the sample complexity scaling of distributions without finite covariance.
